A small non-profit's annual report should tell readers why the organisation exists, what it did, what it learned, how it used money, who is responsible, and how it keeps people safe. This checklist lists those parts in plain terms, so any small group can check its own report before sharing it.

You do not need a large budget or a design team. You need honesty, structure and a willingness to be questioned.

## The purpose and context

- **Mission in one or two sentences:** say who the organisation serves and why.
- **Where it works:** name the area in general terms, such as a district or city.
- **Who leads it:** list the board or leadership and their roles.
- **The problem in context:** if you quote global facts, label them as global and name the source. For example, the World Health Organization estimates that about 16% of the world's population experiences significant disability. That is context, not an achievement.

## The activities

- **What was actually done.** Describe programs and sessions in ordinary words.
- **Who took part, in general terms.** Avoid identifying individuals without consent.
- **What changed during the year.** Note new activities, stopped activities and why.
- **What was planned but not done.** Naming what slipped builds trust.

> **Practical tip:** Write activities as verbs. "Ran life-skills sessions" is clearer than "enhanced capacity".

## The learning

- **What worked, and how you know:** point to observations, feedback or records rather than feelings.
- **What did not work:** name at least one difficulty.
- **What you will do differently:** a report without changes of course is often a report without reflection.
- **What you still do not know:** say it openly.

## The money

Financial reporting can be simple and still be clear and complete. A reader should be able to see these points.

1. Where funds came from, by type of source.
2. How funds were used, in clear categories.
3. What was left over or is being held for later use.
4. Who checked the accounts, if anyone, and how.

If any of these are not yet in place, say so, and say what you plan to do. Never guess or round numbers to look better.

## The people

- **Staff and volunteers, described in general terms.** Explain their roles and how they are supported.
- **Training.** Say what training exists for people who work with vulnerable groups.
- **Decision-making.** Explain who decides what.

## Safety, consent and dignity

For work with children, people with disabilities and older people, this section is essential.

- **Safeguarding approach.** State how the organisation protects people and how concerns can be raised.
- **Consent for stories and images.** Explain your rules.
- **Language.** Use respectful, person-first or identity-first wording that people prefer, and avoid pity.
- **Privacy.** Say how personal information is handled.

Our article on [why consent matters in non-profit storytelling](/blog/article-why-consent-matters-in-nonprofit-storytelling) explains why this belongs in every report.

## The comparison and the future

- **Compare with last year, if there is one.** Say what is the same and what differs.
- **Plans for the coming year.** Label them clearly as plans.
- **How readers can respond.** Give a contact route and invite questions.

## Making the report easy to use

A report nobody can open or understand has not really been shared. Small choices help.

- **Use plain headings.** Readers should find the money, the people and the learning without hunting.
- **Keep files light and readable.** Choose a format that opens on a phone, since many readers do not use a computer.
- **Explain terms once.** Spell out abbreviations and technical words the first time they appear.
- **Offer other ways to read it.** Where possible, provide a short summary, a translation or a spoken explanation for people who find long documents hard.
- **Date it.** Say which period the report covers and when it was published.

## What to leave out, and what to fix later

Being complete does not mean including everything. Leave out personal details about children, families or older people, and anything shared in confidence. Do not add a number just because it sounds good.

Mistakes will happen. Decide in advance how you will handle them.

1. Keep a note of what changed between versions.
2. Correct errors openly, and say what was wrong.
3. Tell people who received the earlier version, where you can.
4. Use the correction to improve next year's process.

> **Good to know:** A visible correction usually builds more trust than a hidden one. Readers know that small organisations make mistakes. They notice how those mistakes are handled.

## The final check before publishing

Before you share the report, read it as a stranger would.

- Are all numbers explained and sourced?
- Are plans clearly separated from results?
- Are limits stated?
- Could any person be identified against their wishes?
- Is the language plain enough to translate easily?
- Is there a clear way to ask questions?
