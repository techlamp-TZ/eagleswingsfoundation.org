Measuring outcomes responsibly means counting only what you can explain, saying what your numbers do not show, and never letting a figure replace the person behind it. A small number that is clearly described is worth more than a large number nobody can check.

This article explains that idea, and how the Foundation plans to think about it.

## Why do big, vague numbers cause problems?

A large total looks impressive. But a total with no explanation invites more questions than it answers. Was one person counted once or many times? Did "helped" mean a conversation, a workshop or long-term support? Over what period? Compared with what?

When these answers are missing, readers cannot tell good work from good marketing. That is unfair to honest organisations too, because careful groups look weaker next to inflated claims.

There is a second risk. When a number becomes the goal, people may start to chase the number instead of the need. Work can drift toward what is easy to count and away from what matters.

## What counts as a good number?

A good number is one that a stranger could question and still trust. It usually has four qualities.

1. **A clear definition.** What exactly was counted?
2. **A source.** Who counted it, and how?
3. **A period.** From when to when?
4. **A limit.** What does this number not tell us?

Global figures need the same care. The World Health Organization's World report on disability is useful background for why this work matters. It describes the world. It is not the result of any one organisation, and it should never be presented as one.

## The four things the Foundation says it will report on

The Foundation's [Impact page](/impact) names four areas. Together they aim to give a rounded picture, because no single measure can.

### Reach

Reach is about who the work touches, in general terms: which programs, which groups, roughly how widely. It answers the question "who was this for?" Reach is the easiest thing to count, which is why it should never stand alone. Being reached is not the same as being helped.

### Learning

Learning is what the organisation discovered, including mistakes. What worked, what did not, and what will change. It shows whether an organisation is paying attention. A report with no learning is often a report with no reflection.

### Practice

Practice is about how the work is done: the quality of sessions, the training of people who lead them, the safeguarding habits and the care taken over consent. Good practice matters because good intentions are not enough when working with children, people with disabilities and older people.

### Experience

Experience is what people themselves say about the work: families, caregivers and participants, in their own words and with their permission. It is the hardest area to measure and the most important to listen to. It should be reported with humility, because it is a set of voices, not a score.

## How do the four fit together?

Here is a simple way to see why they need each other.

| Measure | The question it answers | What it cannot tell you alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reach | Who was involved? | Whether it was useful |
| Learning | What did we find out? | Whether people felt respected |
| Practice | Was the work done well and safely? | How many people took part |
| Experience | What did people tell us? | Whether it was the same for everyone |

Read one column and you may be misled. Read all four and you get a fair, if modest, picture.

> **Good to know:** Being honest does not mean having nothing to say. It means saying what you know, what you do not, and how you found out.

## What does responsible measurement look like in practice?

Responsible measurement is as much about behaviour as arithmetic. A few habits help:

- **Ask with consent.** People and families should be told why they are being asked, and be free to say no without losing support.
- **Protect privacy.** Report in general terms. Avoid details that could identify a child, a family or an older person.
- **Respect dignity.** Use person-respectful language and describe abilities and support, not deficits.
- **Avoid comparing unlike things.** A change in one group does not prove the same change in another.
- **Do not claim more than you know.** Correlation is not proof. Say "we observed" rather than "we caused".
- **Correct mistakes openly.** If a figure was wrong, say so and fix it.

These habits reflect the spirit of the UN [Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities.html), which is built on dignity, autonomy and participation. People are not data points. They are partners in the work.

## What should you expect from a young organisation?

The Foundation was founded in 2025. A young organisation should expect to have little to show at first, and should say so. What you can fairly ask is how it plans to measure its work, what it will do when results are mixed, and whether it will share what it learns.

Be cautious of any group that reports large results very early, without explaining how they were counted. Patience is part of trust on both sides.
