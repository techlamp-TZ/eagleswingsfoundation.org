A sensory-friendly space is one where sound, light, touch, smell and crowds are managed so nobody is overwhelmed. Use the checklists below at home, at school and at community gatherings. You do not need to do everything. Start with one or two changes and notice what helps.

## What is sensory overload?

Our senses take in a lot of information all the time. Many autistic people, and some people with ADHD or other differences, experience sensory input more strongly, or sometimes less strongly, than others. A hand dryer may feel painful. A clothing label may feel unbearable. A busy market may feel like too much all at once.

When this happens, a person may cover their ears, cry, run away, freeze or become very upset. This is a natural response, not bad behaviour. The goal is to reduce triggers and give people ways to recover.

> **Good to know:** Sensory needs differ a great deal. One child may dislike noise but love strong hugs. Another may seek movement and dislike sitting still. Observe the individual.

## Home checklist

### Sound

- [ ] Turn off the TV or radio when nobody is watching or listening.
- [ ] Use soft furnishings, such as rugs or curtains, to reduce echo.
- [ ] Warn your child before loud sounds, such as a blender or a knock at the door.
- [ ] Keep a quiet place where the child can go to rest.

### Light and space

- [ ] Use natural light where you can, and avoid flickering bulbs.
- [ ] Keep the child's play or study area tidy and simple.
- [ ] Put up a picture routine chart so the day feels predictable.

### Touch, clothing and food

- [ ] Let your child choose soft, comfortable clothes. Cut out itchy labels if needed.
- [ ] Introduce new foods slowly, without pressure or shame.
- [ ] Offer a safe, familiar food alongside new ones.
- [ ] Respect a child's dislike of certain textures, and talk with a health professional if eating is a worry.

### Calm and comfort

- [ ] Create a calm corner with cushions, a blanket and favourite items.
- [ ] Offer movement breaks, such as walking, stretching or carrying light items.
- [ ] Keep bedtime and mealtime routines steady.

## School and learning checklist

- [ ] Seat the child away from noisy doors, windows or fans if this helps.
- [ ] Give instructions in short steps, with pictures where possible.
- [ ] Allow movement breaks and quiet breaks.
- [ ] Warn about changes, such as a visitor, a fire drill or a new timetable.
- [ ] Let the child use tools that help, such as headphones or a fidget item, if agreed with the teacher.
- [ ] Teach classmates that everyone senses the world differently.

## Community and event checklist

Whether you are planning a church gathering, family celebration, school event or community day, ask yourself:

- [ ] Is there a quiet space people can retreat to?
- [ ] Can music and speakers be kept to a comfortable level?
- [ ] Can we share the plan for the day in advance, with simple pictures or words?
- [ ] Are there clear signs and a calm way to find toilets and exits?
- [ ] Can people arrive early, or leave early, without judgement?
- [ ] Have we thought about strong smells, flashing lights and crowded queues?
- [ ] Are volunteers and hosts ready to respond with patience and kindness?

## When you are out and about

Even with good planning, some places are hard. A few small habits help.

1. **Visit at quieter times,** when the market or clinic is less crowded.
2. **Prepare your child** with a short, simple description of where you are going.
3. **Bring comfort items,** such as a favourite toy, snack or water.
4. **Agree a signal** your child can use when they need a break.
5. **Leave without guilt** if it becomes too much. Try again another day.

## Sensory-friendly is good design for everyone

Many changes that help autistic children also help older people, people with hearing loss, people who are tired or anxious, and people living with dementia. This idea is part of *universal design*, which means designing spaces that as many people as possible can use. The Centre for Excellence in Universal Design offers helpful information on the idea.

## A note on safety and professional help

This checklist is general guidance. If sensory difficulties cause serious distress, affect eating, sleep or safety, speak to a doctor or qualified therapist. They can offer advice suited to your child.
