A community event is clear and safeguarding-ready when three things are settled before the day: who does what, what people can expect, and what happens if something goes wrong. This guide explains those ideas in plain language. It is general planning guidance, not legal advice, and it does not describe any specific event.

## Why do clarity and safeguarding belong together?

Confusion is one of the biggest risks at any gathering. When nobody knows who is in charge, who is watching the children, or where to go if someone feels unwell, small problems grow quickly.

Safeguarding is the habit of protecting people from harm. It matters most for children, older people and people with disabilities, who may depend on others for support. The World Health Organization estimates that [about 1.3 billion people, around 16% of the world's population,](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/disability-and-health) experience significant disability. That means almost any community gathering will include people with a range of support needs, whether or not anyone mentions them.

Clear planning is how that care becomes practical. It also reflects the principles of the UN [Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities.html): dignity, participation and inclusion.

## What should be decided before the event?

Start with a short written plan. It does not need to be long. It needs to be shared with everyone who is helping.

### Purpose and audience

Write one or two sentences on why the event is happening and who it is for. This keeps the programme focused and helps you judge whether the venue, timing and helpers suit the people you expect.

### Roles and responsibilities

Give every helper a clear role. A simple structure works well:

- **Lead organiser:** makes final decisions and is the main contact.
- **Welfare contact:** looks after anyone who is worried, distressed or unwell.
- **Registration helpers:** welcome people and record only necessary details.
- **First-aid contact:** a person who is trained, or a link to a trained professional.
- **Floating helpers:** support people, guide them around the space and keep walkways clear.

### Limits on what you can offer

Be honest about what the event can and cannot provide. If you do not have medical staff, say so. If a space is not step-free, say so. Accurate information lets families choose for themselves.

## How do we communicate clearly with families and carers?

People take part more confidently when they know what to expect. Share information early, in plain words, and offer more than one way to reach you, such as a phone call as well as a message.

Include these points in your invitation:

1. What the event is and who is welcome.
2. Where it is, how to get there and how the venue is laid out.
3. The order of the day, in simple steps.
4. How to ask for support, for example a quiet space, seating or help with mobility.
5. Who to contact before and during the event.

Use short sentences and avoid jargon. If many people speak Kiswahili at home, consider offering the key information in both Kiswahili and English.

> **Practical tip:** Ask people to tell you about support needs in advance, but never make it a condition of attending. Some people will only mention their needs when they arrive, and that is fine.

## What does safeguarding look like on the day?

Good safeguarding is mostly ordinary good practice, done consistently.

### Supervision and safe spaces

Children and people who need support should never be left without a responsible adult. Agree how helpers will share responsibility for areas such as entrances, toilets, food and quiet spaces. Keep exits clear and make sure helpers know where they are.

### Helper conduct

Volunteers and staff should understand that they must treat everyone with respect, avoid being alone and unobserved with a child, and speak up if they see something worrying. Brief them before the event and give them the welfare contact's details. Our article on [responsible volunteering in community work](/blog/article-responsible-volunteering-in-community-work) covers this in more depth.

### Photos and personal information

Ask permission before taking or sharing photos, and make it easy to say no. For children or people who may not be able to give informed consent, seek permission from a parent, guardian or trusted representative. Why this matters is explored in [why consent matters in nonprofit storytelling](/blog/article-why-consent-matters-in-nonprofit-storytelling).

Collect only the information you need, store it privately and delete it when it is no longer required.

## What if something goes wrong?

Plan for problems so that nobody has to invent a response under stress.

- Decide who calls emergency services and who stays with the person who needs help.
- Keep a phone charged and a list of key contacts in one place.
- Agree how helpers report a concern, and who they report it to.
- Write down what happened after any incident, factually and privately.
- Take every concern seriously and never dismiss a child or adult who speaks up.

If there is a concern about a child's safety, it should be passed to the appropriate local authorities or child protection services. Seek qualified advice about the correct process where you live.

## Checking the plan afterwards

After the event, gather the helpers for a short review. Ask what worked, what felt confusing, and what should change. Write down two or three improvements and keep them for next time. The aim is steady progress, not perfection.
