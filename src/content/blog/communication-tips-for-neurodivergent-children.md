Clear communication for neurodivergent children starts with slowing down, using simple words, and showing what you mean as well as saying it. Follow the steps below and treat every gesture, sound or picture as a real message. These are general tips, not therapy, and every child is different.

## Before you begin: what is communication?

Talking is only one form of communication. Children may point, lead you by the hand, draw, use pictures, sign, hum, or use behaviour to tell you something. When we accept all of these as real communication, children feel heard.

The tips below suit many autistic children and many children with ADHD or Down syndrome. Adjust them to the child in front of you.

## Step 1: Get ready to connect

Before speaking, make sure your child can take in what you say.

- Move to their level, so you are not standing over them.
- Say their name, or gently signal that you want to talk.
- Reduce background noise if you can. Turn off the radio or move away from the crowd.
- Do not insist on eye contact. Many autistic children listen well while looking elsewhere.

## Step 2: Use short, clear words

Long sentences can be hard to follow. Say what you mean directly.

- Instead of "Would you like to maybe start thinking about getting ready?", say "Shoes on, please."
- Use the same words for the same routines, so they become familiar.
- Say what to do, not only what not to do. "Walk slowly" is clearer than "Don't run."
- Be careful with jokes and sayings, which some children take literally.

## Step 3: Show as well as tell

Many children understand pictures, objects and actions better than words alone.

1. Hold up the cup when you ask about water.
2. Use simple drawings or photos for daily routines, such as wake up, wash, eat, school.
3. Point, mime or demonstrate the action.
4. Let your child show you as well, by pointing to a picture or object.

## Step 4: Give time to answer

Some children need extra time to process a question and form a reply. Ask once, then wait quietly. Count slowly in your head. If you repeat the question too fast, the child may need to start again.

> **Practical tip:** Offer two choices, such as "Rice or ugali?" Choices are easier than open questions and give your child a sense of control.

## Step 5: Prepare for change

Sudden changes can cause real distress. Help your child know what is coming.

- Tell them the plan for the day, using pictures if helpful.
- Give warnings before a change: "Two more minutes, then we tidy."
- Explain when something unexpected will happen, in calm and simple words.
- Keep a familiar comfort item nearby.

## Step 6: Respond to stress with calm

When a child is overwhelmed, they may cry, shout, run, cover their ears or shut down. This is usually a sign the world feels too much, not bad behaviour.

- Stay calm and lower your voice.
- Move to a quieter place if possible.
- Use very few words.
- Make sure your child is safe.
- Talk about what happened later, when everyone is calm.

Notice patterns. If a certain time, place or sound leads to distress, you can adjust it next time. The [sensory-friendly checklist](/blog/article-sensory-friendly-home-and-community-checklist) in this series can help.

## Step 7: Celebrate every attempt

Praise effort, not only success. "I like how you showed me the picture" builds confidence. Follow your child's interests too. If they love cars, use cars to practise words, turns and play.

## Things to avoid

Some habits can make communication harder:

- Shouting or repeating the same words louder.
- Speaking about the child as if they cannot hear.
- Comparing them with siblings or other children.
- Forcing hugs, eye contact or speech.
- Using threats or shame.

## When to seek extra help

If you are worried about your child's speech, hearing or understanding, speak to a doctor or a qualified speech-language therapist. They can assess and suggest tools that suit your child. Do not wait until you feel sure. Early support is valuable, and asking is never a mistake.
