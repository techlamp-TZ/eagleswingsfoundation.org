If you are worried about a child, an older person or a person with a disability, speak up early. Tell the organisation's leadership, and where a person may be at risk, the relevant local authorities. If someone is in immediate danger, get them to safety and call emergency services. This FAQ answers common questions. It is general guidance, not legal advice, and it does not replace advice from qualified professionals.

## Why is it hard to speak up?

Many people hesitate. They fear being wrong, causing trouble, upsetting a colleague or not being believed. Some worry they will make things worse for the person they want to protect.

These worries are natural. But the World Health Organization explains that child maltreatment and violence against children can seriously harm health and wellbeing, and that harm can continue if nobody acts. A concern shared early gives others a chance to help. A concern kept quiet leaves a person on their own.

## What counts as a concern?

You do not need to be certain. A concern can be:

- something you saw or heard
- something a person told you
- a change in someone's behaviour or mood that troubles you
- unsafe practice by a helper, such as being alone and unobserved with a child
- a person who appears to be neglected, exploited or afraid

If you are unsure whether it is serious enough, share it anyway. Leadership can decide what happens next.

## What should I do first?

### If someone is in immediate danger

Their safety comes first. If you can do so safely, move them away from the danger, then call emergency services. Tell the organisation's leadership as soon as you can.

### If it is not an emergency

1. Stay calm and make sure the person is safe and comfortable.
2. Write down what you saw or heard, as soon as possible, in plain and factual words. Note the date, time and place.
3. Report it to the organisation's leadership or its named welfare contact, as soon as you can.
4. Keep the matter private. Share it only with those who need to know.

## What should I say if a child or person tells me something?

Listen calmly. Let them speak in their own words and at their own pace. Then:

- Thank them for telling you, and tell them they did the right thing.
- Do not ask leading questions, or press for details.
- Do not promise to keep it secret. Explain kindly that you may need to tell someone whose job is to help keep them safe.
- Do not confront the person they are talking about.
- Tell the responsible person and write down what was said.

> **Good to know:** Your job is to listen and pass it on. Working out what has happened is for leadership, qualified professionals and the relevant local authorities.

## Who should I tell?

Start with the organisation's leadership or its named welfare contact. Every organisation working with children, older people or people with disabilities should have one, and should make sure helpers and families know who they are.

Where a person may be at risk of harm, the relevant local authorities should also be involved. This is normally handled by the organisation's leadership, but if you are worried that nothing will be done, you may need to raise it yourself. Because the correct route varies by place, ask leadership for their process or seek qualified advice about how it works where you live. We do not list specific offices or numbers here because they should be confirmed locally.

## What if the concern is about a leader or a helper?

Do not stay silent because of who it is. If the concern involves the person you would normally tell, take it to another leader, someone above them, or where needed the relevant local authorities. Trust in an organisation depends on nobody being above scrutiny.

## Will I get in trouble for speaking up?

A good organisation welcomes concerns raised honestly and in good faith, and protects the person who raised them. You should not be punished for speaking up. If you do not feel safe doing so inside the organisation, seek qualified advice about your options.

## What happens after I report?

Leadership should acknowledge your concern, decide on next steps, involve the relevant authorities where needed, and keep records securely. You may not be told all the details, because of privacy. That does not mean nothing is being done.

## How can organisations make it easier?

- Name a welfare contact and share their details.
- Explain the process to helpers, families and participants.
- Make it clear that concerns are welcome and taken seriously.
- Keep records private and secure.
- Review the process after any concern.
