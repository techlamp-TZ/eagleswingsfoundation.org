Skilled volunteering means giving a professional or practical skill, such as teaching, writing or organising, to support an organisation's work. It can be very valuable when it matches real needs and works within clear limits. It becomes a problem when a volunteer treats a skill as permission to act outside the rules of the organisation or their own profession.

## What counts as skilled volunteering?

Any ability you have built through study, work or experience can be offered. Common examples include:

- Teaching and tutoring
- Writing, translation and communications
- Photography and design, with proper permissions
- Accounting and administration
- Planning and organising events
- Health-related knowledge, shared within agreed limits

The UN Volunteers programme recognises that volunteers bring diverse skills to community development. The key words are diverse and agreed. A good match starts with the organisation, not the volunteer, deciding what is needed.

## How can teaching skills help?

Teachers understand how to explain ideas, hold attention and adapt tasks. In a community setting, those abilities can support classroom-style sessions, reading and writing practice or creative activities.

Two cautions apply. First, learning support for children and adults with disabilities often follows an approach set by trained professionals and families. Volunteers should follow that plan rather than introducing their own methods. Second, be ready to slow down, repeat and adapt. What works in one classroom may not suit a person who learns differently.

## How can communications skills help?

Clear writing, design and social-media skills help a small organisation explain its work and reach supporters. This is a real gift because many non-profits have limited time for communications.

It also carries responsibility. Communications volunteers should:

- Ask the organisation what may be shared and how.
- Never use a person's name, image or story without proper consent.
- Avoid pity-based or "miracle" framing that treats people with disabilities as objects of inspiration.
- Keep every claim accurate. If a number or outcome cannot be verified, do not use it.

Ethical storytelling protects dignity and keeps trust with families and the public.

## What about therapy-adjacent skills?

Some volunteers have training in health, rehabilitation or counselling. Their knowledge is valuable, but this is the area where limits matter most.

Diagnosis, treatment, therapy planning and medication decisions are formal responsibilities. They involve professional registration, accountability and knowledge of the individual person. A volunteer visit does not replace that structure, and it should never be treated as a shortcut.

If you have health-related training, the respectful path is to:

1. Tell the organisation exactly what your qualifications are.
2. Ask what kind of contribution, if any, is appropriate.
3. Follow the organisation's supervision and safeguarding arrangements.
4. Refer any person's individual needs to the qualified professionals already involved.

You might, for example, help with general education for caregivers or organise materials, if the organisation agrees. You should not assess, treat or advise on medication informally.

## Skilled versus general volunteering

| | Skilled volunteering | General volunteering |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | A particular professional skill | Time, company and practical help |
| Agreement | Scope defined in advance | Tasks assigned as needed |
| Main risk | Working beyond agreed limits | Overcommitting time |
| Main value | Fills specific gaps | Builds community and routine |

> **Good to know:** Both kinds of volunteering matter. A dependable volunteer who simply turns up, listens and helps set up can be just as important as one with a professional title.

## Why fit and respect matter

The World Health Organization's report on disability explains that people with disabilities often face barriers in education, health services and daily life. Skilled volunteers can help reduce some of these barriers, for example through learning support or clear information. But they can only do so if their work fits with the wider support already around a person.

The WHO also describes assistive technology as an important area of support. Anyone with knowledge in this field should remember that choosing and fitting devices is a specialised task best guided by qualified providers.

## Tips for skilled volunteers

- Start with listening. Ask what is needed before you offer solutions.
- Share knowledge in a way others can continue without you.
- Be open to being taught by staff, families and the people you support.
- Put agreements in writing where the organisation asks for it.
- Keep your professional boundaries and confidentiality.
- Say no to tasks outside your competence.
