Open data means sharing information in a form that other people can find, read and reuse. IATI, the International Aid Transparency Initiative, is a shared standard that helps organisations publish information about aid in a consistent way. This page answers the common questions in plain language.

## What is open data, in simple words?

Think of a notice board in a village. Anyone can walk up and read it. Open data is the same idea for information: it is published where people can reach it, in a form they can use, without needing special permission.

Open does not mean careless. Some information should never be public, such as personal details about children or families. Open data is about being clear on the things an organisation should explain, like what it does and how it uses funds.

## What is IATI?

IATI stands for the International Aid Transparency Initiative. According to its website, it is a global effort to make information about aid and development easier to see, compare and use. It offers a common standard, which is a shared way of describing things.

Why does a shared way matter? If every organisation describes its work in a different style, comparing them is hard. A common format makes information easier to read for donors, researchers, communities and other organisations.

You can read the details on the [official IATI website](https://iatistandard.org/en/).

## Who is it for?

The standard exists for several groups:

- **Donors and supporters**, who want to understand where support goes.
- **Communities**, who have a right to know about work that affects them.
- **Partners and researchers**, who want to learn from many organisations at once.
- **The organisations themselves**, who benefit from being clear and consistent.

## Does a small non-profit need to publish this way?

No. IATI is a voluntary standard, and it was not built only for large agencies. A young, small organisation can start with something simpler: a clear website, plain-language reports and open answers to questions. Consistent honesty is the foundation. A shared standard is one way to build on it as an organisation grows.

> **Good to know:** Publishing in a technical format does not make an organisation honest. Clear, plain, truthful reporting matters more than any format.

## What can and cannot be shared?

Responsible transparency has limits. The table below shows the general idea.

| Usually suitable to share | Usually kept private |
| --- | --- |
| What programs exist and their purpose | Names and photos of children without consent |
| General information on how funds are used | Health or family details about individuals |
| What was learned, including difficulties | Home addresses and private contact details |
| How to raise a concern or ask a question | Anything a person asked not to be shared |

This balance reflects the dignity and privacy that the UN [Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities.html) puts at the centre. Our article on [why consent matters in non-profit storytelling](/blog/article-why-consent-matters-in-nonprofit-storytelling) goes deeper.

## Why does open data matter to the people served?

Transparency is not only for donors. Families and communities have an interest in knowing how an organisation works, what it promises and how they can raise a concern. Open information helps them ask questions with confidence, and helps organisations stay answerable to the people they exist to serve.

## Does open data prove an organisation is good?

No. Data can be open and still be incomplete, out of date or badly explained. It is a tool for asking better questions, not a certificate of quality.

That is why numbers should be read with care. Our explainer on [measuring outcomes responsibly](/blog/article-measuring-outcomes-responsibly-small-honest-numbers) describes what a good number looks like.

## How can donors expect to be kept informed?

Supporters should be able to understand what an organisation is doing without having to chase it. In general terms, a donor can reasonably expect:

1. **Plain-language reports** that explain the work, the learning and the use of funds.
2. **Stated limits**, including what is not yet known.
3. **A way to ask questions**, and a respectful, timely answer where possible.
4. **Corrections**, if something published turns out to be wrong.
5. **Respect for privacy**, so that people are never exposed to make a report more moving.

These are general commitments about how to communicate. They are not promises about particular dates, amounts or results.

## How can you check an organisation's openness yourself?

You do not need technical skills. Try these simple checks:

- Can you find a report or update without searching for long?
- Does it explain what was done, in ordinary words?
- Does it say what was hard or unfinished?
- Is there a clear contact route?
- Does it treat people with dignity?

If the answers are mostly yes, that is a good sign. If not, ask a polite question and see how it responds.
