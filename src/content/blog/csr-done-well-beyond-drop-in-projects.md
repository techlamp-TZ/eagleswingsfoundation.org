Good corporate social responsibility (CSR) is patient. It listens first, commits over time and lets the community lead. Short, drop-in projects can feel generous to the giver, but they often leave little behind. This piece offers a values-based view of how companies can do better.

## What is a drop-in project?

A drop-in project is a one-time activity planned mainly around the visitor's schedule. Think of a team arriving for a day, doing a task, taking photos and leaving. Nobody in such a story is acting badly. Most people involved want to help.

The problem is one of design. If the plan starts with what the company wants to do, rather than what the community needs, the result may be pleasant but shallow. This is an illustrative picture, not a description of any specific organisation.

## Why can short-term help fall short?

Support for people with disabilities, older people and their families is rarely a single-event matter. Skills, confidence and routines build slowly. Caregivers need steady guidance. Children who benefit from predictable routines can find sudden visitors unsettling.

A one-day visit may also create work for staff and caregivers, who must host, explain and supervise. If the visit has no follow-up, the effort can feel wasted on both sides.

## What does the evidence and rights framework suggest?

The WHO [World report on disability](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241564182) describes how barriers in services, attitudes and environments limit participation. Lasting change usually means tackling those barriers through sustained effort, not through occasional gestures.

The UN [Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities.html) puts participation and dignity at its centre. In practice, that suggests people with disabilities and their families should have a say in what support looks like, rather than being passive recipients.

## What does steadier CSR look like?

### Listen before you plan

Ask the non-profit what would help, and ask how people with disabilities and caregivers see their own needs. Be prepared to hear that the answer is not what you expected.

### Start small and stay

A modest commitment that continues is more useful than a large one that ends. Reliability lets a non-profit plan. It also builds the trust on which better work depends.

### Share skills, not only money

Businesses hold skills in finance, logistics, design, technology and human resources. Sharing these can strengthen an organisation in ways a single gift cannot. See our explainer on [ways organisations can partner](/blog/article-ways-organisations-can-partner-with-us).

### Look at your own doors, too

The International Labour Organization's page on [disability and work](https://www.ilo.org/global/topics/disability-and-work/lang--en/index.htm) reminds us that inclusion also concerns workplaces. A company that supports disability causes while overlooking accessibility in its own hiring and premises is missing part of the picture.

## How should we treat people in the process?

Dignity is not optional. Some simple habits help:

- Do not ask people to perform gratitude for a camera.
- Get informed consent before sharing any photo or story.
- Describe people by their abilities and the support they use, not by pity.
- Accept that some activities are better left unphotographed.

Our legacy article on [why consent matters in nonprofit storytelling](/blog/article-why-consent-matters-in-nonprofit-storytelling) explores this further.

> **Practical tip:** Before a project, ask: "If nobody ever saw this, would we still do it, and would it still help?"

## Is it ever right to do a one-off?

Yes, sometimes. A single event can be valuable when the community asked for it, when it is well prepared and when it fits within a longer relationship. The concern is not the length of an activity but its purpose and follow-through.

A helpful test is whether the people the activity claims to serve would choose it. If you cannot tell, that is a sign to ask.

## Measuring what matters

Companies often want numbers. But the clearest measure of good CSR is whether relationships improve and whether people served say the support is useful. Be wary of impressive figures that cannot be checked. Report what actually happened, including what you learned and what you would change.
