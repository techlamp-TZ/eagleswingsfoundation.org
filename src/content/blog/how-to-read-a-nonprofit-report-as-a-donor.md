To read a non-profit's report well, look for what was done, for whom, how it is known, and what was hard. Then ask short, respectful questions about anything unclear. You do not need special training, only patience and curiosity.

Here is a simple process any donor can follow.

## Step 1: Read the purpose first

Start with the mission. Can you say in one sentence who the organisation serves and why? If you cannot, note that. A report that starts with vague words such as "transforming lives" without saying how is asking you to trust before you understand.

## Step 2: Look at what was actually done

Find the section on activities. Ask yourself:

- Are the activities described in ordinary words?
- Do I understand what a session, service or visit looks like?
- Is it clear who the work is for?

Be careful when a report lists outcomes without describing the work that led to them. The work is the part you are supporting.

## Step 3: Check the numbers

Numbers are useful, but only when explained. For each one, ask these questions.

1. What was counted?
2. Who counted it, and how?
3. Over what period?
4. What does the number not tell us?

Watch for global figures used as if they were the organisation's own results. For example, the World Health Organization estimates that [about 1 in 100 children worldwide](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/autism-spectrum-disorders) has autism. That number describes the world. It is not the achievement of any local group.

### Small numbers are not a weakness

A small, clearly explained number is often more useful than a big, vague one. Be wary of round, impressive totals that come with no detail.

## Step 4: Look for learning and limits

Find where the report says what did not go well. Good reports include this. They also say what will change.

> **Practical tip:** Search the report for words like "challenge", "learned", "changed" and "limit". If none appear, that itself is worth a question.

## Step 5: Follow the money

You do not need to be an accountant. Look for a simple story.

- Where did funds come from?
- How were they used, in categories you can understand?
- Was anything held back for later?
- Did anyone outside the organisation check the accounts?

If the money story is missing or unclear, ask for it. A fair organisation will explain what it can, and will say what it cannot yet do.

## Step 6: Check how people are treated

A report about people with disabilities or older people should respect them. Look for consent, privacy and dignified language.

Be cautious if you see:

- Full names or faces of children with no mention of consent
- Pity language such as "suffering" or "helpless"
- Miracle stories that make people sound like symbols

The [Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities.html) is built on dignity, autonomy and participation. Reports should reflect that spirit. See also our article on [why consent matters in non-profit storytelling](/blog/article-why-consent-matters-in-nonprofit-storytelling).

## Step 7: Ask good questions

Good questions are specific, calm and open. Compare these two.

| Less helpful | More helpful |
| --- | --- |
| "Is this all true?" | "How did you decide what to count in this section?" |
| "Where does the money go?" | "Could you explain how the funds in this category were used?" |
| "Why so little?" | "What do you hope to be able to report on next time?" |

Send your questions in writing if you can, so the answer is clear. Allow time for a reply. Then note the answer, and note the tone.

## How should you read the answers?

An honest answer may be "we do not know yet" or "we made a mistake and this is what we did". These are good signs. Be cautious of answers that change the subject, become defensive or offer only more praise.
