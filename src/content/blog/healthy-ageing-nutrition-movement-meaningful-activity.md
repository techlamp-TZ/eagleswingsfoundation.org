Healthy ageing means staying able to do the things that matter to you for as long as possible. Three habits support this more than most: eating well, moving regularly and taking part in activities that feel meaningful. None of them needs expensive equipment, and all of them can be adjusted to the person.

## What is healthy ageing?

Many people picture healthy ageing as living without illness. The World Health Organization (WHO) uses a broader idea. It focuses on functional ability, which means being able to move around, meet your own needs, keep learning, build relationships and contribute to your community.

This is an encouraging view. A person can live with a long-term health condition or a disability and still age well if they have the support, food, activity and company they need. The United Nations and the WHO have named 2021–2030 the [Decade of Healthy Ageing](https://www.who.int/initiatives/decade-of-healthy-ageing), a call for governments, communities and families to work together on this goal.

## Why do food, movement and purpose belong together?

Each habit supports the others.

- Good food gives the body energy to move and the brain what it needs to stay alert.
- Movement helps appetite, sleep, balance and mood.
- Meaningful activity gives a reason to get up, to prepare a meal or to go for a walk.

When one area slips, the others often follow. An older person who stops cooking may eat less well. Someone who stops visiting friends may move less. That is why looking at the whole day is more useful than treating each habit separately.

## Nutrition: simple, varied and regular

The WHO healthy diet guidance encourages a variety of foods: vegetables and fruit, legumes such as beans and lentils, nuts, whole grains, and moderate amounts of animal foods. It also recommends limiting salt, sugar and unhealthy fats, and drinking enough safe water.

For older people, a few practical points matter:

- **Regular meals.** Appetite can fall with age, so small, frequent meals may work better than a few large ones.
- **Easy-to-eat textures.** If chewing or swallowing is hard, softer foods can help. Ask a dentist, doctor or nurse for advice, especially if the problem is new.
- **Fluids.** Thirst can become less noticeable, so offering drinks through the day is a kind habit.
- **Shared meals.** Eating with others often improves both appetite and mood.

Local, seasonal foods are often the most affordable and the most familiar, which makes them easier to enjoy.

## Movement: gentle, regular and safe

The WHO physical-activity guidelines say adults, including adults living with disability, should do at least [150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/physical-activity) per week. Older adults should also add balance and strength activities, about 3 days a week, to help prevent falls.

"Moderate" means the person can talk but not sing. Walking, gentle dancing, gardening and light housework can all count. Where a person cannot do that much, the WHO advice is to be as active as their abilities and conditions allow, because some activity is better than none.

> **Good to know:** Check with a doctor, nurse or physiotherapist before starting a new exercise routine, especially after illness, a fall, or if the person has heart, joint or balance problems.

## Meaningful activity: what gives the day shape?

Meaningful activity is anything that gives a person a sense of purpose, identity or enjoyment. It is personal. For one person it may be tending a small garden. For another it may be telling stories to grandchildren, singing, sorting beans, praying with a community or advising younger relatives.

Good questions to ask are:

1. What did this person love doing before?
2. What can they still do, with or without a little help?
3. Who do they enjoy spending time with?

Try to avoid taking over tasks because it is faster. Keeping a role, even a small one, protects dignity. You can read more about this in our piece on [keeping older people mentally stimulated and socially connected](/blog/article-keeping-older-people-mentally-stimulated-and-connected).

## How can families and communities help?

In Dar es Salaam and across Tanzania, older people are often supported by extended family and neighbours. That closeness is a strength. A few ways to use it well:

- Share the load, so one caregiver is not doing everything.
- Invite the older person into family meals and decisions.
- Keep walkways clear and well lit to reduce the risk of falls.
- Notice changes in eating, mood, memory or mobility and encourage a check-up with a qualified health worker.

Caregivers need care too. Rest, information and support make it easier to be patient and kind.
