Practising therapy exercises at home can help a child build skills, but only when it is safe and follows the plan of a qualified therapist. The steps below explain how to prepare, practise and stay alert to your child's comfort. If you are ever unsure, pause and ask the therapist.

## Why does home practice matter?

Therapy sessions are usually short and happen only from time to time. Daily life is where children live, play and learn. Gentle practice at home, in familiar surroundings, lets a child use new skills in real situations, with people who love them.

Home practice does not replace the therapist. It is a partnership: the therapist designs and adjusts the plan, and the family carries it into everyday life.

The World Health Organization notes that regular physical activity is good for health, and that children and adolescents should average about 60 minutes of activity each day. Exercises prescribed for a child with a specific condition are different from general activity, so always follow your child's own plan.

## Step 1: Be clear about the plan

Before you start at home, make sure you can answer these questions. If not, ask the therapist.

- Which exercises or activities should we do?
- How should each one look when done correctly?
- How long, how often and at what time of day?
- What equipment, if any, do we need?
- What signs mean we should stop?
- Who do I call with questions?

Ask the therapist to demonstrate with your child and let you try while they watch. A short written or drawn note makes it easier to remember later.

## Step 2: Prepare a safe space

- Choose a flat, clear area with enough room. Remove sharp objects, loose rugs and anything that could trip your child.
- Use a soft, non-slip surface if your therapist recommends it.
- Keep the area calm, with fewer distractions such as loud television.
- Have water nearby, and make sure your child is comfortably dressed.
- Never leave your child alone during exercises. An adult should be close enough to help at all times.

Any activity involving water, climbing, stairs, wheels or balance needs especially close supervision, and only when the therapist has approved it.

## Step 3: Get your child ready

Children take part more willingly when they feel safe and know what is coming.

1. Tell your child what you will do, in simple words.
2. Show a picture, toy or demonstration if that helps them understand.
3. Choose a time when your child is rested and has eaten, not right when they are tired, hungry or upset.
4. Offer a small choice, such as which exercise to start with, so they feel included.

## Step 4: Practise gently and keep it short

- Start slowly and follow the therapist's instructions exactly.
- Use a calm, encouraging voice. Praise effort, not only success.
- Keep sessions to the length recommended. It is fine to end early if your child is tired.
- Take breaks when needed.
- Make it playful where the therapist agrees, with songs, counting games or taking turns.

> **Good to know:** Progress is often slow and uneven. A hard day does not mean failure. Consistency and kindness matter more than perfection.

## Step 5: Watch for warning signs

Stop the activity and contact your child's therapist or doctor if you notice any of the following:

- Signs of pain, or your child says something hurts
- Unusual tiredness, dizziness or breathing difficulty
- Sudden change in colour, strength or behaviour
- Strong distress that does not settle with comfort
- Anything that worries you

If there is an emergency, seek urgent medical help immediately instead of waiting for a therapy appointment.

## Step 6: Note what happens and share it

Keep a simple notebook. After each session, write a few words:

- What we did
- What went well
- What was hard
- Any questions

Bring the notes to your next appointment. Your observations help the therapist adjust the plan to your child.

## What should families avoid?

- **Copying exercises from videos or other children.** Every child's needs differ.
- **Pushing through distress.** Comfort and trust matter.
- **Comparing progress with other children.** Each child develops in their own way.
- **Changing medicines or treatments.** Only a doctor should advise on this.
- **Doing it alone when you are exhausted.** Ask another trusted adult to share the routine.

## Who can help with home practice?

Other family members can learn the routine too. Grandparents, older siblings and neighbours may be able to support, as long as they understand the safety rules and follow the same plan. Share the note and show them the exercises together, ideally with the therapist's approval.

This guide is general education, not medical or clinical advice. Your child's qualified therapist or doctor knows their needs and is the right person to decide which exercises are safe.
