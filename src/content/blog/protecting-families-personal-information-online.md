To protect a family's personal information online, share only what is needed, keep private details out of public posts, ask before sharing anyone else's photo, and use strong privacy settings. This checklist puts those habits in plain language. It is general guidance, not legal advice, and it does not describe the law in any particular country.

## Why does personal information need protection?

Families of children, older people and people with disabilities often share details to get help: names, health needs, routines and photos. That is sensible, but the same details can be misused if they reach the wrong people.

People with disabilities and their carers can face extra risks if private details become public. A person's dignity and safety are part of their rights, and the UN [Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities.html) emphasises respect and autonomy. Privacy is one way of respecting them.

## Checklist 1: Before you post anything

- [ ] Do I have a clear reason for sharing this?
- [ ] Is it about me, or about someone else? If it is about someone else, have I asked?
- [ ] Would this person be comfortable with it in a year, or in ten years?
- [ ] Have I read it as a stranger would, looking for clues about where the person lives, studies or spends time?
- [ ] Am I sure that nothing in the background gives away a location?

If the answer to any of these is no or unsure, wait.

## Checklist 2: Details to keep out of public posts

Avoid sharing these openly:

- home address, street names or landmarks near home
- phone numbers and email addresses
- names of schools, classes or regular meeting places
- medical or diagnosis details
- daily routines, travel routes and times when someone is alone
- identity documents, or anything that shows them

Even when each piece looks harmless, several small pieces together can give a clear picture.

## Checklist 3: Photos and videos

Photos carry a lot of information. A face, a uniform, a street sign or a familiar building can identify a person or place.

- [ ] Ask for permission before taking a photo of another person.
- [ ] For a child, or an adult who may not be able to consent alone, ask a parent, guardian or trusted representative, and also listen to the person.
- [ ] Explain where the photo will appear and who may see it.
- [ ] Respect a no, and never take a photo of someone who is upset or in a private moment such as personal care.
- [ ] Do not add names, or details that could identify someone, unless they have clearly agreed.
- [ ] Remember that people can change their minds, and be ready to delete.

Our article on [why consent matters in nonprofit storytelling](/blog/article-why-consent-matters-in-nonprofit-storytelling) explains this in more depth.

> **Practical tip:** Before posting a group photo, ask yourself if everyone in it has agreed. If you cannot be sure, choose another photo.

## Checklist 4: Accounts and devices

- [ ] Use a strong, different password for each account, and do not share it.
- [ ] Review the privacy settings on social media, so posts are visible only to people you know.
- [ ] Be careful with public group chats, which can include people you do not know.
- [ ] Keep phones locked with a code or fingerprint.
- [ ] Be cautious about links and messages from unknown senders, especially those asking for personal details or money.
- [ ] Log out of shared or borrowed devices.

## Checklist 5: When you give information to an organisation

- [ ] Ask why they need it and how they will use it.
- [ ] Give only what is necessary.
- [ ] Ask who will see it and how it is stored.
- [ ] Ask how you can correct it, or ask for it to be removed.
- [ ] Read the privacy information. You can read ours on the [privacy page](/privacy).

A responsible organisation should collect only what it needs, keep it secure, and share it only with those who must know.

## Checklist 6: For volunteers and helpers

- [ ] Follow the organisation's rules on photos and messages.
- [ ] Do not share names, photos or stories about the people you support on your own accounts.
- [ ] Do not contact children privately online outside the organisation's approved channels.
- [ ] Report any concern about how information has been used.

Read more about the wider role of helpers in [safeguarding basics for community organisations](/blog/article-safeguarding-basics-for-community-organisations).

## What if something has already been shared?

Do not panic. Ask the person who posted it to remove it, explain why, and keep a record of your request. If the person is a child, or someone may be at risk, tell the organisation's leadership and, where needed, the relevant local authorities. See [how to raise a safeguarding concern safely](/blog/article-how-to-raise-a-safeguarding-concern-safely).
