Respectful language about disability starts with one idea: describe the person first, and describe support needs only when they are relevant. The steps below give you simple habits and word swaps you can use at home, at school, in worship and at work.

## Why do words matter so much?

Words carry attitudes. A phrase that sounds harmless to one person can quietly tell another that they are a burden, a patient or a problem to be solved. Over time, these small signals affect how people are treated and how they see themselves.

The World Health Organization estimates that [about 1.3 billion people, around 16% of the world's population,](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/disability-and-health) experience significant disability. That means most of us talk with, work with or live with someone who does. Respectful language is not a niche skill. It is basic good manners for everyone.

## Step one: put the person first

Start by naming the person, then mention disability only if it helps the conversation.

- Say "a person with a disability" rather than "a disabled case" or "the disabled".
- Say "a child who uses a wheelchair" rather than "a wheelchair child".
- Say "a person living with dementia" rather than "a dementia sufferer".

Person-first language reminds listeners that the person is more than a diagnosis. It is the standard in many organisations, including those that work with the United Nations.

## Step two: know that identity-first language is also valid

Some people prefer to lead with the identity word. Many autistic people, and many Deaf people, say they prefer "autistic person" or "Deaf person", because they see it as part of who they are, not something separate to be politely mentioned second.

Both styles are respectful. The best approach is simple: ask, "How do you like to describe yourself?" Then follow their lead. When you must write about a group and cannot ask, our own articles use both forms and explain that both are common.

## Step three: swap loaded words for plain ones

The table below shows common swaps. It is a guide, not a rulebook, and language keeps changing.

| Instead of | Try |
|---|---|
| Suffers from, afflicted with | Has, lives with |
| Victim of, stricken by | Person with, person who has |
| Handicapped, crippled | Person with a disability |
| Special case, special child | Child with additional support needs, or simply the child's name |
| Normal versus abnormal | Typical versus non-typical, or describe the difference directly |
| Confined to a wheelchair | Uses a wheelchair |
| Overcame her disability | Achieved her goal |
| Mentally retarded | Person with an intellectual disability |

Avoid words used as insults, such as calling something "crazy" or "lame" in jokes. They spread stigma even when nobody is targeted.

## Step four: describe abilities and support, not deficits

Compare two sentences. "He cannot speak and depends on others for everything" describes gaps. "He communicates with gestures and pictures and likes support with meals" describes the person and what helps.

When you write or talk about someone:

1. Say what the person can do, enjoys and chooses.
2. Say what support helps them do it.
3. Only mention diagnosis when it matters for the conversation, and never guess at one.

> **Practical tip:** Before you describe someone, ask yourself, "Would they be comfortable hearing this said about them?" If not, rephrase.

## Step five: speak to the person, not past them

A common mistake is speaking to the caregiver or interpreter instead of the person. Look at the person, greet them, and address questions to them. If they need more time or a different way to reply, allow it.

Do not touch a wheelchair, cane or communication device without permission. These are part of a person's personal space. Do not assume someone needs help, either. Offer, and accept a "no thank you" calmly.

## Step six: avoid pity and miracle stories

Two framings do quiet harm. Pity says a person's life is sad. Miracle framing says any ordinary achievement is extraordinary. Both treat disability as the main story.

Instead, treat people like anyone else: congratulate real accomplishments, acknowledge real difficulties and stay curious. Ask about interests, not only about the disability.

## Step seven: keep learning and forgive yourself

Language shifts, and different people have different preferences. If someone corrects you, say thanks and adjust. If you are unsure, ask politely and privately, and do not expect one person to speak for everyone.

This guide is general education, not legal or clinical advice. For questions about a specific person's diagnosis, needs or care, speak with qualified professionals such as doctors, therapists and social workers.
