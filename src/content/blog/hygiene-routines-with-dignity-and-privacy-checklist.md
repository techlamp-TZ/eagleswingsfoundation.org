Good hygiene routines protect health and support confidence, but how we teach them matters as much as what we teach. Use this checklist to build routines that keep privacy, choice and dignity at the centre. It is general education, not medical advice.

## Why does dignity matter in personal care?

Personal care is intimate. A young person who feels embarrassed or rushed may resist, even when they understand the task. Dignity is also a matter of rights. The UN [Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities.html) is built on respect for autonomy and dignity, and everyday care is where those values become real.

Older children and teenagers, including those aged 12 and above, are also growing and changing. They deserve accurate, calm information and appropriate privacy.

## Checklist 1: Before you start

- [ ] Choose a calm time, not when the person is hungry, tired or upset.
- [ ] Prepare everything you need beforehand: soap, towel, clean clothes, toothbrush.
- [ ] Check water temperature with your own hand.
- [ ] Choose a private space and close the door or curtain.
- [ ] Tell the person what will happen, using words, pictures or gestures they understand.
- [ ] Ask who they would like to help, if a choice is possible.

## Checklist 2: During the routine

- [ ] Let the person do as much as they can by themselves.
- [ ] Offer choices, such as which soap or which towel.
- [ ] Ask before touching, and explain each step as you go.
- [ ] Cover parts of the body that are not being washed.
- [ ] Use neutral, respectful words, and never joke about the body.
- [ ] Watch for signs of discomfort, such as pulling away or distress, and pause if needed.
- [ ] Keep the pace slow and steady.

> **Good to know:** Some people find water, textures or smells overwhelming. This is a sensory difference, not stubbornness. Small changes such as softer cloths or unscented soap may help.

## Checklist 3: Building a routine

Routines help because they make the day predictable. Consider the following:

1. Link each hygiene task to an event, such as brushing teeth after breakfast.
2. Keep the order the same each day.
3. Use a picture strip or short written list where helpful.
4. Place items where the person can reach them.
5. Practise one step until it feels easy before adding another.

Our article on [breaking a chore into small teachable steps](/blog/article-breaking-a-chore-into-small-teachable-steps) explains this method in more detail.

## Common hygiene areas at a glance

| Area | What to think about |
| --- | --- |
| Hand washing | Water, soap, rubbing all surfaces, rinsing, drying. Often linked to meals and toilet use. |
| Teeth and mouth | Gentle brushing, a toothbrush the person likes, regular routine. The WHO has fact sheets on oral health. |
| Bathing | Privacy, safe floors, water temperature, adult supervision as needed. |
| Toilet routines | Privacy, comfort, clear steps, and patience with accidents. |
| Clothing and laundry | Clean clothes, sorting, washing, hanging. |
| Hair and nails | Sensory comfort, gentle tools, agreed routines. |

## Checklist 4: Safety and health

- [ ] Never leave a person alone in water if supervision is needed.
- [ ] Keep hot water, razors and chemicals out of reach unless safe use has been taught and agreed.
- [ ] Use non-slip surfaces where possible.
- [ ] Check skin and teeth gently, and mention any concerns to a doctor, nurse or dentist.
- [ ] Follow any advice from your health professional about equipment or positioning.
- [ ] Never use hygiene tasks as punishment or as a way to shame.

## What if there are challenges?

Refusal, distress or repeated accidents are signals. They may point to pain, fear, sensory overload or a need for clearer teaching. Try the smallest possible change first. If the problem persists, or you notice pain, sores, infection or sudden changes, speak with a qualified health professional. Medication, medical treatment and diagnosis are matters for them, not for this checklist.

## Safeguarding and trust

Everyone who supports a young person with personal care should be trusted, respectful and known to the family. Teach young people, in ways they can understand, that their body belongs to them and that they can say "stop". Let them know they can tell a trusted adult if something feels wrong. If you have any worry about a child's safety, the WHO has information on [child maltreatment](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/child-maltreatment), and you can seek help from a trusted local professional or authority.
