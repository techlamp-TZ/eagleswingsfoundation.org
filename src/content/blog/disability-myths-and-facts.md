Most myths about disability are simply untrue, and they lead to fear, hiding and lowered expectations. The short answers below explain what is known and why it matters for families and communities.

## Myth: Disability is rare

**Fact:** Disability is a normal part of human life. The World Health Organization estimates that [about 1.3 billion people, around 16% of the world's population,](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/disability-and-health) experience significant disability. Many disabilities are not visible, and people may acquire disabilities through illness, injury or ageing.

This matters because when we picture disability as rare, we plan schools, paths and services as though it were. When we plan for the reality, everyone benefits.

## Myth: Disability is a punishment or a curse

**Fact:** Disability is not a punishment, a curse or anyone's fault. Causes vary widely and are often not known. Down syndrome, for instance, is caused by an extra copy of chromosome 21. It is not caused by anything a parent did or did not do.

Blame hurts families twice: first by adding guilt, and second by pushing them to hide the person rather than seek support. A family that feels judged is less likely to ask for help.

## Myth: You can catch it, so keep your distance

**Fact:** Autism, Down syndrome, intellectual disability, ADHD and dementia are not contagious. A child with a disability does not put other children at risk. Playing, eating and learning together is safe.

Some conditions do have health needs that call for care, and families should follow advice from qualified health workers. But a disability itself is never a reason for exclusion.

## Myth: People with disabilities cannot learn, work or contribute

**Fact:** People with disabilities learn, work, make friends, hold opinions and contribute to their communities. Support needs vary, sometimes greatly, and the right support makes a real difference.

The International Labour Organization treats decent work for people with disabilities as a matter of rights. Employers and community groups can ask what support helps a person do a task well rather than assuming what they cannot do.

## Myth: Autism looks the same in everyone

**Fact:** Autism is a spectrum. The WHO reports that [about 1 in 100 children worldwide](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/autism-spectrum-disorders) has autism, and that support needs vary greatly. Some autistic people need substantial daily support. Others live independently. Many have strong interests and skills.

"If you've met one autistic person, you've met one autistic person" is a helpful reminder. Avoid guessing what a person can or cannot do from a label. Anyone concerned about a child's development should seek a qualified assessment.

## Myth: Older people with memory loss are just "getting old"

**Fact:** Dementia is not a normal part of ageing. It is a group of conditions that affect memory, thinking and daily life. WHO reports that [more than 55 million people worldwide](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/dementia) live with dementia, and Alzheimer's disease is the most common form.

A person living with dementia still has feelings, history and dignity. If you notice changes in memory or behaviour, encourage a check with a doctor. Do not try to diagnose or treat at home.

## Myth: People with disabilities need pity, or a cure

**Fact:** Most people with disabilities want what others want: respect, friendship, purpose and a fair chance. Pity creates distance. Miracle stories create pressure. Neither is inclusion.

Some people benefit from therapy, education support or assistive tools. That is different from a "cure" narrative. Be careful with anyone who promises miracle treatments, especially if they ask for money. Speak with a qualified health professional first.

## Myth: Inclusion is too costly or too complicated

**Fact:** Some changes do need investment, but many are simple: clear explanations, patience, welcoming attitudes and flexible ways of taking part. Planning for many kinds of people from the start is usually easier and cheaper than changing things later.

> **Good to know:** The UN [Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities.html), adopted in 2006 and in force since 2008, is built on dignity, non-discrimination, participation and accessibility. It treats inclusion as a matter of rights, not charity.

## What can I do when I hear a myth?

Stay calm and share a fact, not a lecture. You might say, "I read that about 1.3 billion people live with disability, so it is quite common." Suggest reliable sources. Most of all, model respect. Use the person's name and speak directly to them. Our [respectful language guide](/blog/article-respectful-language-about-disability-guide) offers wording you can use.
