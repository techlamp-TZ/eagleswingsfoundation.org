A safe swimming or exercise day depends on four things: close supervision, up-to-date medical information, informed consent and a rehearsed emergency plan. This guide answers common questions in general terms. It is not medical or lifesaving advice. Always involve qualified professionals, such as a doctor, therapist, lifeguard or instructor, when you plan activities in or near water.

## Why are swimming and exercise days worth planning carefully?

Physical activity supports health, confidence and enjoyment. The World Health Organization recommends that children and adolescents average about 60 minutes of physical activity a day, and it stresses that people living with disability can benefit too, with adaptations. Water and movement can also be a happy way to spend time with others.

At the same time, water is a risk for anyone, and exercise can be tiring or unsafe for some people. Good planning lets young people enjoy the day, and helps parents and carers feel confident about the arrangements.

## What supervision is needed?

Supervision is the most important safeguard. Water activities need constant, close attention.

- Supervisors should be capable adults who are alert and focused on the participants.
- They should not be looking after food, photos or other tasks at the same time.
- Take advice from a qualified lifeguard or instructor about how many supervisors your group needs.
- Make sure someone always knows who is in the water and who has left.
- Give helpers clear zones and roles, and confirm handovers when they change.

Young people should never be left alone in or near water, even in shallow areas.

## What medical information should we gather?

Ask parents or guardians to share relevant information well before the day, using a simple form. Useful questions include:

1. Are there health conditions, allergies or medicines that affect safety?
2. Does the young person have mobility or communication needs?
3. Are there fears, triggers or sensory needs around water or crowds?
4. Who should we call in an emergency, and is that person reachable?
5. Has a doctor or therapist advised anything about swimming or exercise?

Keep the information private and share it only with those who need it. Never advise anyone to start, stop or change medicine. That is a decision for a doctor.

## How do we handle consent?

Consent has two parts. Parents or guardians should give written permission after they understand the plan. Young people should also be told what will happen, in plain words, and be free to say no, rest or stop at any time.

Never pressure or tease a young person who is nervous about water. Invite them to watch, join for a short time or take part in a different way. Photos and videos need separate permission, and our article on [why consent matters in nonprofit storytelling](/blog/article-why-consent-matters-in-nonprofit-storytelling) explains why.

## What should the emergency plan include?

Write a short plan and make sure every helper has read it.

- A named person who is trained in first aid, and a clear first-aid kit.
- A trained person or lifeguard for water activities, where possible.
- A working phone, a list of emergency numbers and directions to the nearest medical help.
- A way to account for everyone, such as a register at the start, during and at the end.
- A clear signal for gathering the group quickly.
- A plan for informing parents or guardians if there is an incident.

> **Good to know:** Practise the plan with your helpers before the day. A plan that has only been written down is much harder to follow in a real emergency.

## What else should we prepare for?

### The environment

Check the pool or water area beforehand for hazards, depth, slippery surfaces and access for people who use mobility aids. Choose sensible timing to avoid extreme heat, and arrange shade and drinking water.

### Adapting to different needs

People move differently. Offer choices, such as gentle games, floating, walking in the water with support, or seated exercise. Rest is part of the activity. Ask a physiotherapist or therapist for guidance if someone has specific needs. Our articles on [making events sensory-friendly](/blog/article-making-community-events-sensory-friendly) and [accessibility for community events](/blog/article-accessibility-universal-design-checklist-community-events) give further ideas.

### Dignity and privacy

Provide private changing spaces, and respect modesty and comfort. Helpers should follow safeguarding practice, use respectful language and never leave a young person alone with an adult without appropriate oversight.
