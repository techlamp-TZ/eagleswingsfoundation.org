Work is more than a wage. It gives people purpose, routine, connection and a chance to contribute. We believe the path from everyday life skills toward work and income should be open to people with disabilities, on their own terms and with the right support.

## Why should we talk about work at all?

The World Health Organization estimates that [about 1.3 billion people, roughly 16% of the world's population,](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/disability-and-health) experience significant disability. That is a very large group of people with talent, ideas and the wish to take part in community life.

The UN [Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities.html) recognises participation and inclusion as core principles. The International Labour Organization also has a topic area on disability and work. In short, the wish to earn a living, or to contribute through work, is not a special request. It is part of being a citizen.

At Eagle's Wings, our vision includes empowerment and independence: vocational training, independent-living skills and income. This article explains our thinking in general terms.

## How do everyday skills connect to work?

Many workplace abilities begin at home. A young person who waters plants each morning is learning routine and responsibility. Someone who helps fold laundry is practising sequencing and care. Carrying water, sweeping and cooking teach safe use of tools and physical stamina.

| Everyday skill | Work-related foundation |
| --- | --- |
| Following a daily routine | Reliability and time awareness |
| Doing a chore in steps | Following instructions and finishing tasks |
| Asking for help | Communication with supervisors and colleagues |
| Caring for a garden | Patience and responsibility for living things |
| Tidying a shared space | Working alongside others |
| Handling small purchases | Early money understanding |

These are not guarantees, and they are not a checklist that a person must complete before they are "ready". They are simply connections worth noticing. Our article on [building everyday independence](/blog/article-building-everyday-independence-one-skill-at-a-time) explores how such skills grow.

## What does work look like in practice?

Work can take many forms, and there is no single right answer.

- **Employment:** a paid role with an employer, with agreed adjustments.
- **Self-employment:** a small business, such as gardening, craft, tailoring or food preparation.
- **Family enterprise:** taking a real part in a family shop, farm or workshop.
- **Supported activity:** structured work with regular support, in a group or workplace setting.
- **Volunteering and community roles:** contribution that builds skills and confidence.

Every option deserves respect. What matters is choice, safety, fair treatment and, where earning is possible, fair pay.

## What stands in the way?

Barriers are often outside the person. They include low expectations, lack of accessible transport or training, limited information for families, and fear among employers. When we treat disability as only a personal problem, we miss these barriers.

Simple adjustments can help. Examples include clear written or pictorial instructions, a quiet place to work, predictable schedules, or extra time to learn a task. These are reasonable ideas, not legal advice or promises about local rules.

> **Good to know:** Being asked "what helps you do your best work?" is often more useful than any assumption about what a person cannot do.

## What can families and communities do?

1. Give young people real household and community roles early.
2. Notice strengths and interests, and follow them.
3. Speak with local businesses, faith groups and neighbours about inclusion.
4. Help young people practise real tasks with real feedback.
5. Involve the young person in every decision about their future.
6. Seek advice from qualified professionals about training, assessment and safety.

Communities in Dar es Salaam and across Tanzania are rich in trades and small enterprises. Inclusion often begins with one neighbour or business owner willing to welcome a new person and learn together.

## A word of caution

Work should never be forced. Some people will not want to work, or may not be able to for health reasons. Some may need long-term support. Their dignity and value do not depend on earning. A responsible approach avoids pressure, avoids promises about jobs, and keeps the person's wellbeing first.
