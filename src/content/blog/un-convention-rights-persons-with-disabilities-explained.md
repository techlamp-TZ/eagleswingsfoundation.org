The [Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities.html), often called the CRPD, is a United Nations human rights treaty. It was adopted in 2006 and came into force in 2008. Its central message is simple: people with disabilities have the same human rights as everyone else, and society must remove the barriers that stand in the way.

## What is the CRPD?

The CRPD is an international agreement. Countries that choose to join commit to respecting, protecting and promoting the rights it describes. It is one of several UN human rights treaties, and it was created because people with disabilities were often left out of the rights other people took for granted.

The Convention does not invent new rights. Instead, it spells out how existing human rights, such as education, health, work, family life and freedom from harm, apply to people with disabilities. This clarity helps governments, schools, employers and communities know what respect looks like in practice.

## Why was it needed?

The World Health Organization estimates that [about 1.3 billion people, roughly 16% of the world's population,](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/disability-and-health) experience significant disability. Despite this, many people with disabilities have faced exclusion from schools, workplaces, health services and public life, often because of barriers and attitudes, not because of the disability itself.

The CRPD reflects a change in thinking. Older approaches treated disability mainly as a personal medical problem or a matter of charity. The Convention supports a rights-based view: disability arises when a person with an impairment meets barriers in the environment or in attitudes. The task is to reduce the barriers.

## What are its core principles?

The Convention rests on a few big ideas. Here they are in everyday words.

| Principle | What it means in daily life |
|---|---|
| Dignity | Every person has worth, and no one is treated as a burden |
| Autonomy | People make their own choices, with support where needed |
| Non-discrimination | People are not treated worse because of disability |
| Participation and inclusion | People take part fully in community life |
| Respect for difference | Disability is part of human diversity |
| Accessibility | Places, information and services can be used by all |

These ideas overlap. For example, real participation needs accessibility, and real autonomy needs respect for a person's own voice.

## What areas of life does it cover?

The Convention covers many parts of daily life. In broad terms, it addresses:

- education and learning
- health and rehabilitation
- work and employment
- family life and relationships
- access to information, buildings and transport
- protection from violence, abuse and neglect
- taking part in community, cultural and public life

Each area follows the same principle: people with disabilities should have equal opportunities, and reasonable support and changes should be made so that they can use their rights.

## What does "nothing about us without us" mean?

This phrase, widely used by disability rights advocates, captures how the Convention was shaped and how it should be used. Decisions that affect people with disabilities should involve them, along with their families and representative organisations.

It also applies at community level. A school, faith group or neighbourhood association that wants to include people should ask them what is needed and listen to the answers. ## How is the CRPD used in each country?

A treaty sets shared principles. Each country decides whether to join and how to put the principles into national law and practice. This means details differ from place to place. This article is general education and not legal advice. To find out which laws and services apply where you live, consult official government sources or a qualified legal professional.

## Why does the CRPD matter to families?

For families and caregivers, the Convention offers language and confidence. It says that wanting your child to go to school with peers, or your older relative to be treated with dignity, is not asking for a favour. It is asking for rights.

It also supports the idea that families need support too. Caring for someone is easier when services, information and community attitudes are welcoming.

## What can communities do with it?

You do not need to be a lawyer to use the Convention's spirit. Communities can ask simple questions.

1. Who is missing from our activities, and why?
2. What barriers, physical or attitudinal, could we reduce?
3. Are people with disabilities helping to decide what we do?
4. Are we speaking about people with dignity? See our [respectful language guide](/blog/article-respectful-language-about-disability-guide).
