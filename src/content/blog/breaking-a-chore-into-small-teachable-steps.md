To teach a chore, break it into small steps in a fixed order, teach one step at a time, and give only as much help as the person needs. This method is often called task analysis, and families can use it at home with everyday tools like a bucket, a mop or a basin of washing.

## Why break chores into steps?

A chore that looks simple to an adult often hides many decisions. Washing clothes, for example, involves sorting, wetting, soaping, scrubbing, rinsing, wringing and hanging. If we ask a young person to "do the laundry", we may be asking for all of it at once.

Small steps reduce that load. They also make it clear where a person needs help, so support is targeted rather than general. Many autistic people find clear, predictable sequences easier to follow, though every person is different.

## Step by step: how to build a teaching plan

### 1. Choose one chore that has meaning

Pick something useful in your household, such as mopping a floor, washing a few clothes, or watering plants. Real purpose is motivating.

### 2. Do the chore yourself and write the steps

Perform the chore slowly. Write down each action in the order you do it. Use short phrases that start with a verb.

### 3. Check the steps for hidden parts

Ask what the person must already know. Where is the water? How do they know the floor is clean? Add those steps if needed.

### 4. Decide how to show the steps

Options include:

- Spoken words, one step at a time.
- Photographs or drawings placed in order.
- A simple written list for people who read.
- A demonstration with the person watching, then joining.

Choose the option the person understands best. You can also mix them.

### 5. Teach one step, then add the next

Start with one step, practise it until it feels comfortable, and then link the next. Keep sessions short. It is better to finish while things are going well.

### 6. Reduce help slowly

Begin with the level of help that lets the person succeed. That might be hand-over-hand help. Then move to pointing, then a picture, then a quiet reminder, and finally no prompt.

### 7. Celebrate progress

Notice effort, not just perfection. A smile, a thank-you or a shared cup of tea can be enough.

## Example: washing a few clothes

This is a general illustration, not a fixed rule. Change it to suit your home and the person.

1. Collect the dirty clothes in a basket.
2. Put a small amount of water in the basin.
3. Add a little soap and mix.
4. Place one item in the water.
5. Rub the cloth gently between the hands.
6. Rinse the item in clean water.
7. Squeeze out the water.
8. Hang the item on the line.
9. Put the basin away and dry your hands.

Notice that each line is one action. If squeezing is hard because of hand strength, you might teach it with a lighter cloth first, or share the step.

## Example: mopping a small room

1. Move small items off the floor.
2. Fill the bucket with a little water.
3. Wet the mop and wring it.
4. Mop from the far corner towards the door.
5. Rinse the mop when it looks dirty.
6. Let the floor dry, and walk carefully until then.
7. Empty the bucket and hang the mop.

A wet floor can be slippery, so walk slowly and keep the area clear.

> **Practical tip:** Keep one set of steps in the same place, such as beside the washing basin. Consistency helps the person learn without depending on a particular helper.

## What if it is not going well?

Every learner has hard days. Try these ideas:

- Make the step smaller.
- Change the time of day.
- Offer a choice of two tools or two tasks.
- Check for noise, heat or discomfort.
- Take a break and return later.

If a person seems distressed often, or you have worries about their health or behaviour, speak with a doctor, therapist or other qualified professional. This article offers general education only.

## Keeping it dignified

Teach in private if the person prefers. Speak to them as you would to any peer. Avoid comparing them with siblings or neighbours. Chores are shared work, and a young person who contributes is a valued member of the household.
