A partnership usually starts with a simple conversation about needs and purpose, moves through questions and planning, and is then written down in a clear agreement. This guide describes that path in general terms. It is not a description of any one organisation's process, and it is not legal advice.

## Step 1: Clarify your own reasons

Before contacting a non-profit, be clear about why you want to partner. Do you want to share a skill? Support a particular kind of work? Involve your staff?

Write down what you can offer and what you cannot. Honesty here saves everyone time. If the main goal is publicity, it is better to say so early, so that it can be discussed openly.

## Step 2: Make a first contact

Reach out in a simple, respectful way. Introduce your organisation, explain your interest and ask whether a conversation would be welcome. Avoid arriving with a finished plan. A non-profit may have limited time, so be patient about replies.

Many organisations list contact routes on a website. For Eagle's Wings Foundation, those are on the [Contact page](/contact).

## Step 3: Listen to the needs

The most valuable early conversation is about what would help. Ask questions like:

- What are your current priorities?
- What is hardest to resource at the moment?
- How do people with disabilities and caregivers shape your plans?
- What should we avoid doing?

The UN [Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities.html) emphasises participation. A good partner keeps that in mind when listening.

## Step 4: Check that you fit together

Both sides should look at values, safeguards and capacity. Our [partner due-diligence checklist](/blog/article-partner-due-diligence-checklist-both-sides) gives questions to work through. Take time. The World Health Organization estimates that [about 16% of people worldwide live with significant disability](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/disability-and-health), so many people may be affected by how well a partnership is built.

## Step 5: Agree a small starting point

Rather than committing to everything, agree on a modest first step. This might be a short activity, a skills session or a limited contribution. It lets both sides learn how they work together before promising more.

> **Good to know:** A gradual start is a strength, not a weakness. Trust is built through small things done reliably.

## Step 6: Write down what was agreed

A written agreement records shared understanding. It can be formal or simple, depending on the size and nature of the partnership. Topics that agreements commonly cover, in general terms, include:

1. **Purpose:** what the partnership aims to achieve.
2. **Roles:** who does what, and who decides.
3. **Contribution:** what each side offers, whether goods, skills, time or funds.
4. **Timing:** start points, review points and how long it lasts.
5. **Communication:** contact people, how often you will talk and how you will handle disagreements.
6. **Safeguarding and consent:** expectations for protecting people, and for photos, stories and personal information.
7. **Use of names and images:** how each side's name and materials may be used, and who approves this.
8. **Reporting:** what will be shared and in what form, in the spirit of openness supported by initiatives such as the [IATI Standard](https://iatistandard.org/en/).
9. **Review and ending:** how to check progress, make changes or bring the partnership to a respectful close.

Your own agreement may include more or fewer points. The details of any legal terms depend on the parties and the place, so please take advice from a qualified legal professional before signing anything binding.

## Step 7: Start, review and adjust

Once underway, check in regularly. Ask what is working, what is difficult and what should change. Be ready to adapt. If something is not helping the people it is meant to serve, be willing to stop or rethink.

Keep records of what happens, and report honestly. Our article on [transparent reporting as programs grow](/blog/article-transparent-reporting-as-programs-grow) explains why this matters.

## What if it does not go ahead?

Sometimes the right outcome is that a partnership is not the right fit. That is fine. A respectful conversation that ends without an agreement is better than an uneasy agreement that causes harm. You may find another way to help later.
