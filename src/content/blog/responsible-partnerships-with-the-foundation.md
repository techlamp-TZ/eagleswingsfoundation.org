A responsible partnership is one where both sides put the dignity and wellbeing of people with disabilities first, agree openly on what each will do, and report honestly on what happens. It is less about the size of a gift and more about the quality of the relationship. This article sets out the values we believe such a partnership rests on.

## Why does the way we partner matter?

The World Health Organization estimates that [1.3 billion people, about 16% of the world's population](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/disability-and-health), experience significant disability. Many of them, along with their families and caregivers, need steady support rather than one-off gestures.

When organisations work together, they can reach further than either could alone. But partnership also carries risk. A well-meant project can create dependence, put pressure on families or use people's stories in ways they never chose. The way we partner decides whether the outcome is respectful or harmful.

## Start with shared purpose

Every good partnership begins with a simple question: what are we both trying to achieve? For Eagle's Wings Foundation, the purpose is to empower, encourage and educate people with special needs, less privileged people and older people by helping, caring for and supporting their needs.

A partner does not need to share every detail of that work. But they should share its spirit. If a proposal mainly serves a partner's image, and only loosely serves the people involved, it is worth pausing and talking again.

## Put dignity and rights first

The UN [Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities.html) rests on dignity, autonomy, non-discrimination, participation and respect for difference. These ideas can guide everyday decisions in a partnership.

- **Dignity:** people are described by their abilities and support needs, never with pity.
- **Autonomy:** people and families are asked, not assumed for.
- **Participation:** people with disabilities and caregivers help shape what is offered.
- **Respect:** activities are planned so that no one is put on display.

In practice, this means partners agree that a visit, a photo or a story is never more important than someone's comfort and consent.

## What does responsible support look like?

Support is most useful when it fits real needs and can be sustained. Some questions help test this:

1. Does this contribution answer a need that has actually been described, rather than one we assumed?
2. Can it continue, or will it stop when our attention moves elsewhere?
3. Does it build the skills of local people, or does it replace them?
4. Does it avoid creating extra work or pressure for families and caregivers?

> **Good to know:** Support does not have to be money. Skills, materials, expertise and time can all help, as long as they are matched to real needs and agreed in advance. See our explainer on [ways organisations can partner](/blog/article-ways-organisations-can-partner-with-us).

## Be honest in reporting

Trust grows when organisations tell the truth about what they have done, including what did not work. Plain reporting is better than polished claims. It should name what was agreed, what was delivered, and what remains uncertain.

Aid transparency efforts such as the [IATI Standard](https://iatistandard.org/en/) show that openness about resources and activities is increasingly expected across the sector. Even small organisations can follow the same principle by keeping clear records and sharing them.

We hold ourselves to the same rule on this website: no invented numbers, no invented stories. A partner should expect us to say "we do not know yet" when that is the honest answer.

## Protect people first: consent and safeguarding

Children, older people and people who rely on others for care can be vulnerable to being overlooked or exposed. A responsible partnership therefore treats consent and safeguarding as ground rules, not extras.

- Anyone visiting or volunteering agrees to follow safeguarding expectations.
- Photos and stories are shared only with informed, freely given consent.
- Activities such as swimming or exercise are supervised and guided by suitably qualified people.
- Concerns can be raised by anyone, without fear of blame.

Our related article on [why consent matters in nonprofit storytelling](/blog/article-why-consent-matters-in-nonprofit-storytelling) explains this in more detail.

## Keep it steady, and keep it free

The best partnerships are patient. They give time for trust to grow, for plans to adjust, and for lessons to be learned. They also leave room for either side to say no, to change course or to end the relationship respectfully.

Nobody should feel trapped by an agreement, and nobody should feel pressured into accepting something that does not serve the people at the centre of the work.
