A welcoming community is built from many small habits. Use this checklist to look honestly at how your school, faith group or neighbourhood association includes people with disabilities, older people and caregivers, then choose a few things to improve.

## How to use this checklist

Work through it with a small group, ideally including people with disabilities and their families. Tick what you already do, and mark what you could try. Do not aim to tick everything at once. Choose two or three changes, act, and revisit in a few months.

The World Health Organization estimates that [about 1.3 billion people, about 16% of the world's population,](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/disability-and-health) experience significant disability. Whatever your community's size, some of those people are likely already part of it, or would join if the door were more open.

## Part one: people and attitudes

Attitudes shape whether someone feels welcome before they ever reach the door.

- [ ] We greet people with disabilities and their caregivers by name and speak to the person directly.
- [ ] Leaders and volunteers know respectful language. See our [guide to respectful language](/blog/article-respectful-language-about-disability-guide).
- [ ] We ask what support is helpful instead of assuming.
- [ ] Children and adults are taught that difference is normal and that bullying and teasing are not accepted.
- [ ] Families are never blamed or shamed for a person's disability.
- [ ] We keep personal information private and ask permission before sharing photos or stories.
- [ ] We know who to speak to if someone is being treated badly.

## Part two: spaces and information

Simple planning can make places usable by more people. The idea of universal design, which means designing things to work for as many people as possible from the start, is a helpful guide.

- [ ] Entrances, paths and seating have been looked at by people who use wheelchairs, walking aids or need to rest often.
- [ ] There is a quiet, calmer space for anyone who finds noise, crowds or bright light overwhelming.
- [ ] Important information is given in plain words, spoken and written, and repeated where needed.
- [ ] Signs are clear and simple, and pictures are used where helpful.
- [ ] Seating is flexible, and people can move or stand if they need to.
- [ ] Toilets and water points are safe and reachable.
- [ ] Emergency plans consider people who may need extra time or help to leave.

> **Practical tip:** Walk through your space as if you had a pushchair, a walking stick or a child who dislikes loud sounds. You will spot barriers quickly.

## Part three: activities and roles

Access is not only about getting in. It is about taking part and contributing.

### For schools

- [ ] Teachers explain tasks in more than one way, such as showing, telling and doing.
- [ ] Children are given time and different ways to show what they know.
- [ ] Peers are encouraged to include one another in play, sport and group work.
- [ ] Families and teachers share information regularly and respectfully.
- [ ] Staff seek guidance from qualified professionals about individual learning needs.

### For faith groups

- [ ] Services and gatherings are welcoming to people who move, speak or respond differently.
- [ ] People with disabilities are invited to take part in real roles, such as welcoming, music, reading, serving or organising, if they wish.
- [ ] Older people, including those living with memory loss, are visited and included with patience and dignity.
- [ ] Caregivers are offered support and understanding, not only prayers.

### For neighbourhood associations

- [ ] Meetings are held at times and places that most people can reach.
- [ ] Notices are shared in several ways, including by word of mouth for people who do not read easily.
- [ ] People with disabilities and older residents are asked for their views on local plans.
- [ ] Neighbours are encouraged to look out for one another, respectfully and without taking over.

## Part four: voice and follow-up

- [ ] People with disabilities, older people and caregivers help make decisions.
- [ ] We have a simple way for anyone to share ideas or concerns.
- [ ] We choose one or two changes at a time and check whether they helped.
- [ ] We celebrate progress and learn from what did not work.
- [ ] We remember that we are not experts in medical care, and we direct people to qualified health, therapy and social work professionals when advice is needed.

## What if we can only do a little?

That is fine. A community that starts with three good habits is further along than one that plans everything and starts nothing. Begin with listening, add one physical or communication change and choose one activity to make more welcoming.
