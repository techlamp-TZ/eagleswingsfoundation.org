To keep a useful daily observation log, write a few short, factual notes at the same time each day: what happened, when, and what helped. Keep it brief and use the same headings each day. In a few weeks you will have a clear record that a doctor, nurse or therapist can read quickly.

## Why keep a log at all?

When you visit a professional, it is hard to remember the last few weeks. Under pressure, people forget details or mix up days. A log solves this. It also helps you see patterns you might miss, such as a hard afternoon that always follows a poor night's sleep.

A log is not a test and it is not a diagnosis. It simply records what you saw. The professional decides what it means.

## Step by step: how to start

### 1. Choose a notebook or a place

Use a school exercise book, a phone note or a simple folder. Pick one place only. Keep it where you can reach it easily, and keep it private.

### 2. Pick four or five headings

Choose only what matters for this person. Common headings include:

- Sleep
- Meals and drinks
- Mood and energy
- Activity and rest
- Anything different today

You can change the headings later. Start small.

### 3. Set a fixed time

Choose a time when you are calm, for example after the evening meal. A habit is easier to keep when it is tied to something that already happens every day.

### 4. Write facts, not conclusions

Compare these two entries:

| Less helpful | More helpful |
|---|---|
| "Bad day, refused everything." | "Refused breakfast. Ate lunch when sitting with the family. Cried after the visitors arrived." |
| "Was aggressive." | "Pushed the cup away and shouted after the radio was switched on." |
| "Slept badly." | "Awake several times in the night. Up early." |

The second column tells the reader what actually happened, and what was going on around it.

### 5. Note what helped

If something calmed the person, write it down. A familiar song, a quiet room, a walk or a favourite food might be the most useful line in the whole log.

### 6. Mark questions

Keep a small section for questions you want to ask at the next visit. Write them as they come to mind, so you do not forget them.

## What should you not write?

Some things do not belong in the log:

- **Guessed diagnoses.** Leave those to qualified professionals.
- **Blame.** Avoid words that judge the person or the family.
- **Medicine changes.** Never change medicine on your own. If you have concerns about medicine, contact the doctor or nurse who prescribed it.
- **Unnecessary private details.** Write only what helps with care.

Use respectful words. Write "the person refused" or "the person became upset", not labels such as "difficult" or "naughty".

> **Practical tip:** If writing feels like too much, use ticks and one-word notes. Consistent small notes are better than long entries you cannot keep up.

## How do you share the log with professionals?

Before an appointment, read through the recent pages and mark the main points. You might say, "Over the last few weeks, sleep has been broken most nights, and afternoons seem hardest." Then hand over the log so the professional can look at the details.

Ask the person for their agreement where possible. A person with a disability or an older person has a right to privacy and to be involved in decisions about their own information. Explain what you are writing and why.

Also consider who else might use the log:

- Family members who share care can add notes, so nothing is lost when different people are present.
- Teachers or therapists may find it helpful if the person agrees.
- Keep the book in a private place and do not share it outside the care team without permission.

## How do you keep going?

Many logs fail after a week because they were too big. To keep going:

1. Reduce the number of headings.
2. Link the log to a daily habit.
3. Share the task with another family member.
4. Skip a day without guilt and simply continue the next day.

If the log itself becomes a source of stress, simplify it. It should support you, not add to your load. Our article on [why caregivers need support too](/blog/article-why-caregivers-need-support-too) explores this further.

## Where does the log fit in wider care?

The log works alongside a steady daily pattern. If you are building one, see [supporting families with consistent care routines](/blog/article-supporting-families-with-consistent-care-routines). A routine gives structure to the day, and the log shows how the day actually went.

If you are unsure when a concern is serious enough to raise, read [when and how to seek professional guidance](/blog/article-when-and-how-to-seek-professional-guidance-faq). In an emergency, contact emergency services or the nearest health facility straight away instead of writing in the log.
