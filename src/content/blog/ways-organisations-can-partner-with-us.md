Organisations usually partner with a non-profit in one of four ways: by giving goods or services (in-kind), by sharing professional skills, by providing sponsorship or funding, or by encouraging employees to volunteer. Each can work well when it matches a real need and is agreed clearly. This guide explains what each involves and how to think about the choice.

## Why are there different types of partnership?

Non-profits need many things at once. Some needs are practical, like materials for a classroom. Others need expertise, such as help with planning or communication. Others need steady funding so that programs can continue.

Because organisations also differ, partnership comes in different shapes. The World Health Organization notes that [about 16% of people worldwide experience significant disability](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/disability-and-health). Meeting such wide needs usually takes many kinds of contribution, working together.

## In-kind support

In-kind support means giving goods or services instead of money. Examples include materials for learning activities, equipment for exercise sessions, or printing services.

**Good fit when:** the non-profit has described a specific need and your organisation can supply it reliably.

**Take care:** unrequested donations can create storage, sorting or safety problems. Equipment for people with disabilities should be suitable for the person using it. The WHO's page on [assistive technology](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/assistive-technology) explains why a good match between a person and a device matters. Always ask before sending anything.

## Technical and professional skills

Some partners give expertise instead of goods. This might be help with accounting, design, translation, training materials or digital tools. Skills partnerships can strengthen an organisation for years.

**Good fit when:** your team has a skill the non-profit lacks and can commit time to see the task through.

**Take care:** skills should be shared in a way that leaves local people more capable, not more dependent. Agree who owns the finished work and who will maintain it. Health, therapy or clinical guidance should only come from suitably qualified professionals.

## Sponsorship and funding

Sponsorship means providing money for an activity, a period of work or general running costs. It can be a one-time gift or a repeated commitment.

**Good fit when:** your organisation prefers to let the non-profit decide how best to use resources, or wants to support a defined activity.

**Take care:** agree in advance what the money is for, how it will be reported, and how any recognition will be handled. Funding that arrives with too many conditions can distract from the work itself.

## Employee volunteering

Some organisations encourage staff to give time. Volunteers might help with events, share a skill or support community activities. The [United Nations Volunteers](https://www.unv.org/) programme shows how widely volunteering is recognised as a way to contribute.

**Good fit when:** the non-profit has tasks that suit short or occasional help, and there is capacity to prepare and supervise volunteers.

**Take care:** volunteers working near children, older people or people with disabilities must follow safeguarding rules, and should not be placed in roles that need professional training. Our article on [responsible volunteering in community work](/blog/article-responsible-volunteering-in-community-work) covers this in more depth.

## How do the four types compare?

| Type | What is given | Best when | Main thing to agree |
|---|---|---|---|
| In-kind | Goods or services | A specific need exists | Suitability and delivery |
| Skills | Professional expertise | A gap in know-how exists | Ownership and follow-through |
| Sponsorship | Funding | Steady support is needed | Purpose and reporting |
| Employee volunteering | Staff time | Tasks suit short-term help | Safeguarding and supervision |

Many partnerships combine two or more types. For example, a partner might sponsor an activity and also share a professional skill.

> **Practical tip:** Before proposing anything, ask the non-profit what would help most, and be honest about what you can keep doing over time.

## How do you choose?

Three questions can help.

1. **What is the need?** Start with what the non-profit says it needs, not with your preferred gift.
2. **What can we sustain?** A smaller commitment kept for a long time usually helps more than a large one that fades.
3. **Who benefits, and how are they treated?** Confirm that the plan respects people's dignity, privacy and consent.
