Responsible volunteering means giving your time in a way that serves the people you support, not your own need to feel helpful. It is reliable, respectful and honest about its limits. In community work with children, older people and families, that approach protects dignity and builds trust.

This is a values-based view from the Eagle's Wings Editorial Team. It is not a story about a particular volunteer. It is a description of the habits we believe make volunteering safe and worthwhile.

## Why does "good intentions" fall short?

Almost every volunteer starts with a kind wish. Kindness is a good beginning, but it can go wrong when it ignores what people actually want and need.

A person with a disability is not a project. An older person living with dementia is not an audience for your generosity. Each person has their own history, preferences and rights. The UN [Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities.html), adopted in 2006 and in force since 2008, is built on dignity, autonomy, non-discrimination, participation and inclusion. Responsible volunteering puts those ideas into everyday behaviour.

## Values that guide responsible volunteers

### Dignity first

Speak to the person, not about them. Ask before you help. Offer choices instead of taking over. Help that removes someone's say in their own day is not really help.

### Reliability

People plan around you. A family may arrange their morning because a volunteer said they would come. If you cannot keep a commitment, say so early and clearly. Small, steady contributions are worth more than dramatic ones that fade.

### Humility

You are joining work that others have shaped. Listen before you suggest changes. Expect to learn from staff, families and the people you support, who know their own needs far better than a newcomer does.

### Honesty about limits

Know what you are trained for and what you are not. If a situation feels beyond your skill, stop and ask a coordinator. Guessing is not brave. It can be unsafe.

## What volunteers do and do not do

The table below is a simple guide. Your organisation's own rules always come first.

| Volunteers can usually support | Volunteers should leave to trained people |
|---|---|
| Company, conversation and shared activities | Diagnosis or medical judgement |
| Practical help such as set-up, tidying and carrying | Medication decisions of any kind |
| Encouraging routines and joining group sessions | Therapy planning and clinical treatment |
| Sharing skills the organisation has asked for | Legal, safeguarding or social-work decisions |

> **Good to know:** If someone tells you something that worries you, do not investigate on your own or promise to keep it secret. Pass it to the responsible coordinator straight away.

## Why does responsible volunteering matter for this community?

The World Health Organization estimates that [1.3 billion people, about 16% of the world's population,](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/disability-and-health) experience significant disability. Families and caregivers often carry much of the daily support. Community volunteers can lighten that load, but only when their help fits with the care plans, routines and wishes of the family and the professionals involved.

Unplanned or careless help can cause confusion, break a helpful routine or leave someone feeling like an object of pity. Careful help does the opposite. It makes a person feel included and respected.

## Habits you can practise from day one

- Arrive on time and ready to listen.
- Ask what the person prefers and accept the answer.
- Use respectful language. Say "person with a disability" or "person living with dementia" rather than words that define someone by a condition.
- Respect privacy. Do not share names, photos or personal details without clear permission from the organisation and the person or their family.
- Follow supervision instructions, especially around water, exercise and outings.
- Reflect afterwards. Ask yourself what went well and what you would do differently.

## Volunteering is a relationship, not a favour

The strongest volunteering feels like a partnership. The volunteer brings time and willingness. The organisation brings structure, training and safeguards. The people supported bring their own knowledge of their lives. Take away any one of these and the arrangement weakens.

The UN Volunteers programme describes volunteering as something that benefits both the community and the volunteer. That is a healthy way to think about it, provided the community's needs are named first.
