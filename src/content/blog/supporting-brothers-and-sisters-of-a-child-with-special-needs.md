Brothers and sisters of a child with special needs are children too, with their own hopes, worries and need for attention. We believe a family is strongest when every child is seen, heard and valued equally. This article shares a perspective on how families can support siblings with honesty, fairness and warmth.

## Why do siblings deserve their own attention?

When one child needs extra care, therapy visits or close supervision, family time and energy are stretched. That is understandable. Yet a brother or sister may quietly notice they are asked to be patient, to wait or to understand more than most children their age.

This is not a criticism of any parent or caregiver. Most families do their best with limited time. But to say it clearly: every child in the family has equal value. The rights ideas behind the UN [Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities.html), such as dignity, respect for difference and inclusion, begin at home, in how we treat each other every day.

## What might a sibling be feeling?

Every child is different, but many siblings experience a mix of feelings at once.

- **Love and pride.** Many siblings are fiercely protective and proud of their brother or sister.
- **Confusion.** They may not understand why their sibling behaves or learns differently.
- **Frustration.** Shared toys, noise or routines can feel hard.
- **Feeling left out.** Time and attention may seem to go elsewhere.
- **Worry.** They may fear for their sibling's health or their parents' tiredness.
- **Embarrassment.** Especially among friends or in public, and often followed by guilt.

None of these feelings is wrong. Naming them helps children know they are normal.

## Talking honestly, at the right level

Children fill silence with their own ideas, which are sometimes scarier than the truth. Simple, honest explanations are kinder.

### Use plain words

For a younger child, you might say: "Your brother's brain works in a different way, so some things are harder for him and some things are easy. He still loves to play with you." Older children can be told more as they ask.

### Invite questions, and it is fine not to know

You do not need every answer. "That is a good question. Let us ask the therapist together" shows children that curiosity is welcome.

### Speak respectfully about the disability

The words families use become the words children use. Focus on what the child can do and what support helps them. Avoid pity or phrases that suggest the child is a burden.

> **Good to know:** Families use different words for describing disability, such as "person with a disability" or, for autism, "autistic person" or "person with autism". Listening to the words your family member prefers is a respectful place to start.

## Protecting a sibling's childhood

### Time of their own

Even a short, regular moment matters: a walk, a game, helping with dinner or a chat before sleep. The message is, "You matter to me for who you are, not for how you help."

### Helping, but not as a full-time carer

Small tasks can build love and responsibility. But brothers and sisters should not become the main carer. Children need time to play, learn, rest and make friends.

### Their own interests and achievements

Celebrate school, sport, art and friendships. It is easy for good news to be overlooked during demanding weeks. Let siblings share it with pride.

## Helping siblings become friends and allies

Siblings often build unique bonds. You can nurture these by:

1. Finding shared activities that both children enjoy, such as music, drawing or games outside.
2. Teaching a sibling simple ways to communicate that work for their brother or sister, if a therapist has suggested them.
3. Showing that differences are valued, not hidden.
4. Letting siblings share their views when family decisions are made, in ways that suit their age.
5. Welcoming their friends into the home so they do not have to choose between worlds.

## When is extra support needed?

Watch for signs that a brother or sister might be struggling: withdrawing, unusual anger, trouble sleeping, changes at school, headaches or stomach aches with no clear cause, or seeming very anxious. These signs do not always mean something serious, but they are worth a gentle conversation. If they continue, consider speaking to a doctor, teacher, counsellor or social worker.

Extended family and community can also help. In many Tanzanian families, aunts, uncles, grandparents and neighbours are part of daily life. A trusted relative can offer one-to-one time to a sibling and give parents a moment to breathe.

This article offers a general perspective. It is not counselling or clinical advice.
