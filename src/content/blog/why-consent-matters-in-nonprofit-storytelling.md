Consent matters in nonprofit storytelling because the people in the story own their own lives. A photo, a video or a personal account is never just content. It is a piece of someone's identity, and they should decide if it is shared. Real consent is informed, freely given and open to withdrawal.

This is a values-based view from the Eagle's Wings Editorial Team. It describes how we believe organisations should behave. It is general guidance, not legal advice.

## Why is storytelling risky for nonprofits?

Stories move people. That is exactly why they are tempting. A moving image can bring attention and support to a cause. But the person in that image did not choose to become a symbol. They may be a child, an older person living with dementia, or a person with a disability who has faced being talked about instead of listened to.

The UN [Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities.html), adopted in 2006 and in force since 2008, is built on dignity, autonomy and respect for difference. Autonomy includes the right to decide how your image and story are used. If a story takes that choice away, it works against the values the organisation says it holds.

## What does real consent look like?

Consent is more than a signature. A form can be signed under pressure, without understanding, or by someone who felt they could not refuse. Real consent has four qualities.

### It is informed

The person knows what will be shared, for what purpose, and where it may appear, such as a website, a social media page or a printed leaflet. Explain in plain language and in the language the person uses at home.

### It is freely given

Nobody should feel that saying yes is a condition for support, or that saying no will change how they are treated. Ask at a calm moment, not on the day of an activity in front of others.

### It is specific

Agreeing to one photo for one purpose is not agreement to every future use. If the plan changes, ask again.

### It can be withdrawn

A person can change their mind. A responsible organisation makes it easy to say so, acts quickly, and does not make the person feel guilty.

## What about children and people who may not be able to consent alone?

Some people cannot give full, informed consent on their own. This includes children and some adults, for example an older person living with advanced dementia or a person with an intellectual disability. In these cases, ask a parent, guardian or trusted representative, and explain everything just as clearly.

Their permission is necessary, but it is not the end of the matter. The person themselves should still be asked in a way they can understand, and their reaction matters. If a child pulls away from the camera or a person seems upset, stop. A guardian's yes does not cancel a person's obvious no.

> **Good to know:** If you are not sure who has the right to give consent for someone, do not guess. Ask the organisation's leadership and, where needed, seek qualified advice.

## Dignity in the story itself

Consent is only the first step. The story also has to be told well.

- Show people as whole persons with abilities, interests and choices, not only as needs.
- Avoid pity language, such as "suffering" or "helpless", and avoid "miracle" stories.
- Use "person with a disability" and "person living with dementia".
- Never pair a person's image with claims about their diagnosis or private life unless they have clearly agreed.
- Keep out anything that could identify or endanger them, such as home addresses, school names or daily routes.

The World Health Organization warns that children can face violence and harm in many settings. Careless publicity can add to risk, especially where a child's location or circumstances are easy to work out.

## What if consent is unclear?

The rule is simple: if consent is missing, unclear or doubtful, do not publish. A missed opportunity costs very little compared with harm to a person's privacy or safety. Stock images, illustrations, photos of activities where no one is identifiable, and general descriptions written without personal details can all tell a fair story.

## Why this is about trust, not only rules

Families entrust organisations with people they love. That trust is fragile. When a family sees that permission was asked, respected and easy to withdraw, they can relax and take part fully. When they see the opposite, they may stop coming.

Good storytelling also produces better stories. When people take part willingly, the result feels warm, respectful and true to life, and readers can tell.
