To make an event sensory-friendly, reduce sudden or overwhelming sound, light and crowding, share a clear plan in advance, and provide a quiet place where people can take a break. These steps help autistic people and others who find busy environments tiring. This guide gives general planning ideas. It is not clinical advice, and every person's needs are different.

## What do we mean by "sensory-friendly"?

Many people experience the world through senses that can feel louder, brighter or more intense than others expect. This is common among autistic people. The World Health Organization notes that [about 1 in 100 children worldwide](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/autism-spectrum-disorders) has autism, and that it is a wide spectrum where support needs vary greatly. Both "autistic person" and "person with autism" are used, and it is polite to ask which someone prefers.

Sensory-friendly does not mean the same thing for everyone. One person may find music painful, another may be happiest near a window with fresh air. The aim is to offer choices and reduce avoidable stress.

## Step 1: Ask and learn before you plan

Start by asking. Talk to autistic people, parents, carers and support workers about what helps. Invite comments in your invitation, but do not force anyone to explain personal details.

Listen without judging. Accept that advice may differ, and plan options rather than one fixed solution.

## Step 2: Choose the venue and layout carefully

The place shapes how the day feels.

1. Visit the venue at the same time of day as your event, and notice the noise, echo, smells and light.
2. Choose spaces with natural light and airflow where possible, and avoid harsh flickering lights.
3. Keep walkways wide to avoid crowding and bumping.
4. Place loud activities, such as music or games, away from the quiet area.
5. Keep entrances calm, with a helper ready to greet and guide people.

## Step 3: Make the day predictable

Uncertainty can cause stress. Predictability gives people a sense of control.

- Share the programme in advance, with times and a simple map of the venue.
- Use a visual timetable with pictures and short words, displayed at the entrance.
- Explain what will happen at the start, such as speeches, food or music.
- Warn people before loud or sudden moments, such as clapping, drums or announcements.
- Keep the schedule steady and flag changes gently if they happen.

> **Practical tip:** A short "what to expect" sheet, sent before the event, helps first-time visitors. Include photographs of the venue only if you have permission and they show no people.

## Step 4: Create a quiet space

A quiet space is often the most valuable change. It should be a calm area where anyone can rest without being questioned.

- Choose a spot away from the noise, with dim or soft light.
- Provide seating, cushions or mats, and keep it uncluttered.
- Mark it with a clear sign and let people know they can use it at any time.
- Do not use it as a place of punishment or isolation. It is a choice, not a consequence.
- Make sure a helper checks in from a respectful distance, especially if children are alone there.

## Step 5: Brief helpers to be calm and flexible

Helpers set the tone. Brief them before the event.

- Speak calmly, use short sentences and give people time to respond.
- Avoid touching people or their belongings without permission.
- Understand that a person covering their ears, leaving the room or repeating actions may be managing stress, not misbehaving.
- Offer choices rather than orders, such as "Would you like to sit here or near the window?"
- Know where the quiet space, the welfare contact and the exits are.

For more on preparing helpers, see [responsible volunteering in community work](/blog/article-responsible-volunteering-in-community-work).

## Step 6: Adapt activities and food

- Offer activities that people can join at their own pace, with the option to watch first.
- Reduce background music or turn it low, and avoid sudden loud sounds.
- Provide simple, plain food and drink options, and label them clearly.
- Allow people to bring familiar objects, headphones or comfort items.
- Keep the programme short enough to avoid fatigue, with breaks built in.

## Step 7: Review with the people who came

Afterwards, ask kindly what helped and what did not. Invite honest replies and accept that you will not get everything right the first time. Write down changes for next time and keep improving. Guidance on asking respectfully is in [collecting event feedback without exploiting stories](/blog/article-collecting-event-feedback-without-exploiting-stories).
