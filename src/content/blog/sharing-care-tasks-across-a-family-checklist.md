Sharing care across a family works best when every task is named, every relative knows their part, and the plan is reviewed often. Use this checklist as a starting point for a family conversation. Adapt it to your own home, because every family is different.

## Before you begin: three ground rules

Sharing care can bring out old tensions. These rules help.

- **Keep the person at the centre.** The plan is about their wellbeing, dignity and wishes.
- **Assume goodwill.** Most relatives want to help, but they may not know how.
- **Aim for fair, not identical.** People have different jobs, health, money and time.

The UN [Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities.html), adopted in 2006 and in force since 2008, is built on dignity, autonomy and participation. In practice, that means the person is a partner in the plan and not just its subject.

## Checklist part one: prepare the conversation

- [ ] Choose a calm time, not in the middle of a crisis.
- [ ] Invite everyone who helps or wishes to help, including relatives who live elsewhere by phone.
- [ ] Ask the person receiving care what matters to them, and how they would like to be supported.
- [ ] Agree that everyone speaks and everyone listens.
- [ ] Decide who will write things down.

## Checklist part two: list every task

Many caregivers do far more than others realise. Writing the tasks down makes the work visible.

### Daily personal care

- [ ] Washing, dressing and grooming
- [ ] Meals and drinks, including preparing and feeding if needed
- [ ] Toileting support
- [ ] Reminders for medicine that a doctor or nurse has prescribed

### Movement, rest and activity

- [ ] Gentle exercise or walks that a professional has said are suitable
- [ ] Supervised activities, especially anything involving water
- [ ] Time for hobbies, learning or talking
- [ ] Quiet rest

### Household and practical tasks

- [ ] Shopping, cooking and cleaning
- [ ] Laundry
- [ ] Transport to appointments or school
- [ ] Paperwork and keeping health records

### Emotional and social support

- [ ] Companionship and conversation
- [ ] Visiting or phoning regularly
- [ ] Taking the person to community or faith gatherings, if they wish

## Checklist part three: match tasks to people

Use a simple table like this on a sheet of paper.

| Task | Who | How often | Backup |
|---|---|---|---|
| Morning routine | | | |
| Meals | | | |
| Appointments and transport | | | |
| Paperwork | | | |
| Evening visit or call | | | |
| Rest time for the main caregiver | | | |

Some help does not involve hands-on care. A relative who lives far away might handle phone calls, research or a contribution to costs. Another might take over for a few hours so the main caregiver can rest.

> **Practical tip:** Ask each person, "What can you do reliably?" A small task done every week is worth more than a large promise that is soon forgotten.

## Checklist part four: agree how you will work together

- [ ] Decide how relatives will share news, such as a family message group or a notebook in the home.
- [ ] Keep a shared record. Our article on [keeping a simple daily observation log](/blog/article-keeping-a-simple-daily-observation-log) explains how.
- [ ] Agree that everyone uses the same calm approach, so the person is not confused by different rules.
- [ ] Decide who speaks to doctors, therapists or teachers, and who else attends.
- [ ] Agree how to handle emergencies, and keep important phone numbers in one place.

## Checklist part five: protect the caregiver

- [ ] Make sure the main caregiver has regular time off.
- [ ] Ask honestly how they are coping.
- [ ] Notice signs of exhaustion, low mood or illness, and encourage them to seek help.
- [ ] Say thank you. Recognition matters.

Read more in [why caregivers need support too](/blog/article-why-caregivers-need-support-too).

## Checklist part six: review the plan

Needs change. A child grows, an older person's health shifts, a relative moves for work.

- [ ] Choose a date to review the plan.
- [ ] Ask what is working and what is not.
- [ ] Change one thing at a time.
- [ ] Bring in professional advice when the person's needs change. Our [FAQ on seeking professional guidance](/blog/article-when-and-how-to-seek-professional-guidance-faq) can help you decide when.

This checklist is general guidance. It does not replace advice from doctors, nurses, therapists or social workers, who can advise on the needs of the individual.
