Companies and NGOs considering a partnership often ask the same questions: who can partner, what can we give, and how do we start? The short answer is that many kinds of organisation can help, in many ways, provided the work is respectful and clearly agreed. Below are plain answers to the questions we expect most often.

## Who can be a partner?

Businesses of any size, other non-profits, community groups, schools, professionals and institutions can all be partners. You do not need to be large or well known.

What matters is shared purpose. The World Health Organization estimates that [about 16% of the world's population experience significant disability](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/disability-and-health), and older people and caregivers also need support. There is room for many kinds of contribution.

## What can we offer?

Common forms of contribution include:

- **Goods or services** that meet a described need.
- **Professional skills** such as design, accounting or training.
- **Funding** for an activity or for running costs.
- **Time**, for example through staff volunteering.

We describe these in our explainer on [ways organisations can partner](/blog/article-ways-organisations-can-partner-with-us). Many partnerships combine more than one.

## Do we need to give money?

No. Money is helpful, but it is not the only way to contribute. A steady offer of skills or time can be just as valuable. The important thing is to ask what is needed and to offer only what you can keep doing.

## What should we expect to be asked?

A responsible non-profit will ask about your purpose, your values, how you handle safeguarding, and what you hope to gain. Expect questions about how you would use its name and any photos or stories.

These questions are a sign of care. They also work in your favour, because you can ask the same of us. Our [partner due-diligence checklist](/blog/article-partner-due-diligence-checklist-both-sides) lists questions for both sides.

## Can we visit, take photos or share stories?

Visits, photos and stories involve real people, often children, older people and people who rely on others for care. Their dignity and privacy come first.

The UN [Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities.html) is built on respect for dignity, autonomy and participation. In practice this means informed, freely given consent, and a willingness to accept that some things will not be shared. Our article on [why consent matters in nonprofit storytelling](/blog/article-why-consent-matters-in-nonprofit-storytelling) explains more.

> **Good to know:** Partners should never expect access to people as a right. Visits and images are agreed carefully and in advance.

## Is a one-day project welcome?

Sometimes, if it responds to a real request and is well prepared. But steady, patient support usually helps more than a single event, and it is easier on families and caregivers. Our perspective piece on [doing CSR well](/blog/article-csr-done-well-beyond-drop-in-projects) looks at this in more detail.

## How does a partnership start?

Usually with a simple conversation. You introduce your organisation, listen to what is needed, check that you fit together, and then agree a modest first step. Later, the agreement is written down. We describe this in general terms in [how a partnership starts and what an agreement covers](/blog/article-how-a-partnership-starts-and-what-an-agreement-covers).

That article is general guidance. It is not a statement of legal terms, and binding agreements should be reviewed by a qualified legal professional.

## Will we see results?

We prefer honesty over big promises. We will not claim results we cannot support, and we ask partners to do the same. Good reporting tells you what was agreed, what was done and what was learned, including what did not work.

## What about the people we serve, who are they?

Eagle's Wings Foundation works to improve the lives of people with disabilities and challenges, especially children and older people, and supports their families and caregivers. Support for older people includes those living with dementia. The WHO notes that [more than 55 million people worldwide live with dementia](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/dementia), which shows why family and caregiver support matters. You can learn more about our [programs](/programs).
