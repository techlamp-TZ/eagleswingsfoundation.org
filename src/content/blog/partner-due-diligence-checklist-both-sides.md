Before two organisations agree to work together, both should check that their purpose, values, safeguards and capacity fit. This is called due diligence, and it works best when it goes in both directions. The checklist below gives companies and non-profits a shared set of questions to ask.

## Why should both sides check?

Companies want to be sure their support is used well. Non-profits want to be sure a partner will treat the people they serve with respect. Each side carries responsibility, so each side has a right to ask questions.

Good due diligence is not a sign of distrust. It shows care, and it usually makes the relationship stronger. Ask in a friendly way, explain why you are asking, and be ready to answer the same questions yourself.

## Checklist 1: Purpose and values

- Do we both understand what the partnership is meant to achieve?
- Are our values compatible, especially on dignity and the rights of people with disabilities?
- Does the plan reflect the principles of the [UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities.html), such as dignity, participation and non-discrimination?
- Is the aim to help people, and not mainly to gain publicity?

## Checklist 2: Safeguarding and consent

People who depend on others for care can be at greater risk of harm. The World Health Organization's information on [violence against children](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/violence-against-children) is a reminder of why protective steps matter.

- Is there a written safeguarding approach, and do all involved understand it?
- Will anyone who meets children, older people or people with disabilities follow it?
- How will consent be handled for photos, stories and personal information?
- Is there a clear, safe way to raise a concern?

Our article on [preparing community events with safeguarding in mind](/blog/article-preparing-community-events-safeguarding-ready) offers related ideas.

## Checklist 3: Transparency and records

- Can each side explain, in plain language, how resources are received and used?
- Are records kept, and can relevant information be shared on request?
- Does reporting state its limits and unknowns, not only successes?
- Are there conflicts of interest that should be disclosed?

The [IATI Standard](https://iatistandard.org/en/) is an example of how the wider sector approaches openness about resources and activities. A small organisation does not need complex systems, but it should be able to explain what it does clearly.

## Checklist 4: Capacity and fit

- Does each side have the time, people and skills to do what it promises?
- Is the timeline realistic, and can the work continue after the first burst of energy?
- Will the partnership avoid overloading families, caregivers or staff?
- Is the offered contribution actually wanted and useful?

## Checklist 5: Communication and branding

- Who speaks for each organisation, and how are decisions made?
- How will each side's name, logo and words be used, and who approves this?
- Will people with disabilities and their families be portrayed with dignity?
- How often will we check in, and how will disagreements be handled?

> **Good to know:** A partner that welcomes these questions is usually a good sign. Be cautious if questions are avoided or if someone insists on speed.

## Checklist 6: Exit and review

- Can either side pause or end the partnership respectfully?
- Is there a point where we review how things are going?
- What happens to shared materials, data and commitments if the relationship ends?
- Is there a plan so that people who rely on the work are not left suddenly without support?

## How to use this checklist

Treat it as a conversation guide. Go through it together, note where answers are not yet clear, and decide who will find out more. Some answers will only become clear over time.

If a question touches contracts, taxes, employment or other legal matters, ask a qualified professional. This checklist is general and educational, and does not replace legal or financial advice.
