Transparent reporting shows readers what an organisation really did, what it learned and where it struggled. It is not the same as impressive reporting. As programs grow, the report should get clearer and more complete, not just bigger.

This is a values-based view from the Eagle's Wings Editorial Team. It is not a report of results. The Foundation, founded in 2025 in Tegeta, Dar es Salaam, does not currently publish numeric results, and this article does not claim any.

## Why does growth make reporting harder?

A very small organisation can explain its work in a few sentences. Everyone who cares about it can ask questions directly. When programs multiply, that closeness fades. Supporters, families and partners can no longer see everything for themselves.

At that point the report has to do the seeing for them. It must be organised, consistent and readable by people who were not in the room. If it is not, gaps fill with assumptions, and assumptions are often more flattering than the truth.

## What should a growing organisation show?

A useful report answers a few plain questions. Each one below is a promise to the reader.

### What did we do?

Describe the activities in ordinary words. Say who took part in general terms, where the work happened and what a session or service actually looked like. Avoid vague phrases such as "empowered communities" unless the report explains what that means in practice.

### What did we learn?

Learning is more useful than applause. A good report says what worked, what did not, and what will change. It admits when a plan had to be adjusted, and why.

### What are the limits?

Every program has boundaries. A report should say what the organisation does not do, what it cannot promise, and where it depends on other professionals such as doctors, therapists and social workers.

### How was money used?

Readers deserve a clear description of where funds came from and how they were spent, in categories they can follow. The point is not perfection. The point is that the story adds up.

## What does good measurement look like?

Numbers can help, but only when they are clearly explained. A small number that is clearly explained is worth more than a large number nobody can check. A good report says what was counted, how it was counted and what the number cannot tell us.

It also keeps facts and hopes apart. "We plan to" is not the same as "we did". Readers should never have to guess which one they are reading.

Global context can be quoted, but it must be labelled as global context. For example, the World Health Organization estimates that about 16% of the world's population experiences significant disability. That figure describes the world. It says nothing about what any single organisation has achieved.

## How should mistakes and changes be handled?

Growth involves mistakes. A session may be too crowded. A plan may not fit a family's real life. A message may be worded badly.

Transparent organisations say so. They explain the change and what they did about it. This does not weaken trust. It shows that someone is paying attention.

> **Good to know:** A report that never mentions a difficulty is not proof that there were none. It is a reason to ask more questions.

## How does dignity fit into reporting?

Reporting about people with disabilities and older people carries extra responsibility. Reports should never expose individuals for the sake of a good story. Consent matters, privacy matters, and respectful language matters.

The [Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities.html) is built on dignity, autonomy and participation. A report that treats people as material for fundraising works against those ideas. A report can be moving without being intrusive. Our related article on [why consent matters in non-profit storytelling](/blog/article-why-consent-matters-in-nonprofit-storytelling) explains this in more detail.

## Can open standards help?

Yes. Some organisations publish their information in common, shared formats so that others can compare and check it. The International Aid Transparency Initiative offers one such standard, mainly used in international aid. Even organisations that do not use it can borrow its spirit: publish clearly, publish consistently, and let people look for themselves.

## What should a reader do?

Readers have a role too. Ask for plain answers. Notice when a report avoids a question. Compare reports from year to year, and look for whether the organisation says what it learned. Trust is built when both sides take part.

## What this means at Eagle's Wings

The Foundation is at an early stage. It would rather say so than pretend otherwise. Its [Impact page](/impact) sets out the four things it intends to report on: reach, learning, practice and experience. It will not describe results it cannot honestly support.

When reporting becomes available, it will be published on this website. If you have a question about how the Foundation works, please [contact us](/contact) and ask.
