The best volunteer role is one that uses what you are good at, fits the time you have and matches what the organisation needs. Work through these steps in order and you will avoid the common mistake of promising more than you can deliver. The people you support rely on you being there.

## Step 1: List what you can offer

Take a sheet of paper and write down your skills. Include the obvious ones, such as teaching, cooking or driving, and the quieter ones, such as patience, listening, tidiness or being calm under pressure.

Do not limit yourself to your job. Someone who runs a household well may be excellent at organising a busy activity space. Someone who is a good listener may bring real comfort to an older person who enjoys company.

## Step 2: Be honest about your time

Many volunteers begin with enthusiasm and then run out of hours. Look at your week as it really is: work, study, family duties, travel time in Dar es Salaam traffic, rest.

Ask yourself:

- How many hours can I give regularly, without stress?
- Are my free times predictable, or do they change every week?
- Could I still show up during a busy month?

Choose a pattern you can keep for a long stretch, not just a few weeks. Consistency matters because many people supported by community work, including children and older people, feel more secure with familiar faces.

## Step 3: Know your limits and your comfort zone

Everyone has limits. Some people find hospital-like settings hard. Some are not comfortable with physical support such as lifting. Some are still learning how to speak respectfully about disability.

None of this rules you out. It simply shapes where you begin. Be plain with the coordinator about what you are not ready for. Roles that involve personal care, physical exercise or water activities need proper training and supervision, so never take these on informally.

## Step 4: Ask what roles exist

Do not assume what the organisation needs. Ask. Roles change with programs and seasons, and a helpful idea for one group may be unnecessary for another.

You can read about current opportunities on the [volunteers page](/volunteers) and learn what the [programs](/programs) involve so your questions are informed. Then contact the team through the [Get Involved](/get-involved) page.

## Step 5: Compare roles side by side

If more than one option appeals, a short comparison helps.

| Question | Role A | Role B |
|---|---|---|
| Does it use a skill I enjoy? | | |
| Can I keep the schedule? | | |
| Is training available? | | |
| Who supervises me? | | |
| Do I feel comfortable with the tasks? | | |

Fill in your own answers. Whichever role gets the most confident answers is usually the best start.

> **Practical tip:** If you are unsure, start with the smaller commitment. It is easy to add hours later. It is much harder to repair trust after a dropped promise.

## Step 6: Prepare and begin

Once you agree on a role, get the basics clear. Find out where to go, who to report to, what to wear and what the safety expectations are. Learn the respectful language the organisation uses when speaking about people with disabilities and older people. Ask what is confidential and what you must not photograph or share.

## Step 7: Review and adjust

After a few sessions, pause and reflect. Are you enjoying the work? Are you being useful? Do you feel supported? Talk with your coordinator. It is normal to change tasks, reduce hours or take on more once you understand the work.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Choosing a role because it sounds impressive rather than because it fits.
- Ignoring travel time and rest.
- Staying silent when a task feels unsafe or beyond your training.
- Treating volunteering as a way to fix people instead of a way to support them.

## Why fit matters so much

The World Health Organization estimates that [1.3 billion people, about 16% of the world's population,](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/disability-and-health) experience significant disability. Families and caregivers often provide most of the daily support. When volunteers are well matched and dependable, they can give those families some breathing space. When volunteers are poorly matched or unreliable, they can add pressure.

The UN Volunteers programme presents volunteering as a two-way exchange that benefits communities and volunteers alike. Choosing a good fit is how both sides gain.
