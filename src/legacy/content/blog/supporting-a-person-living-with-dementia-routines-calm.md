A person living with dementia can feel safer and calmer when days follow a familiar pattern, people speak clearly and kindly, and the surroundings are quiet and uncluttered. This checklist collects practical ideas for families and caregivers. It is general information and does not replace advice from a doctor, nurse or social worker.

## What is dementia, briefly?

Dementia is a term for a group of conditions that affect memory, thinking, mood and the ability to manage daily tasks. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), [more than 55 million people worldwide](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/dementia) live with dementia, and Alzheimer's disease is the most common form, making up 60–70% of cases.

Each person is different. Two people with the same diagnosis may have very different abilities and needs. Getting a qualified assessment matters, because some causes of memory change can be treated. Only a health professional can diagnose dementia or advise on medicines.

## Checklist 1: Build a gentle daily routine

Routine gives structure when memory is unreliable.

- [ ] Wake, eat, wash and rest at similar times each day.
- [ ] Keep the order of familiar tasks the same, such as tea, a wash, then a walk.
- [ ] Plan the busier tasks for the time of day when the person is usually most alert.
- [ ] Leave room for rest, since tiredness can increase confusion.
- [ ] Include something enjoyable every day, such as music, a chat or time outside.
- [ ] Introduce changes slowly, and explain them simply.

## Checklist 2: Communicate clearly and kindly

How we speak often matters more than what we say.

- [ ] Approach from the front, make eye contact and say the person's name.
- [ ] Use short sentences and one idea at a time.
- [ ] Ask simple questions, ideally with two choices, such as "Tea or juice?"
- [ ] Give time to answer without rushing or finishing sentences.
- [ ] Use gestures, pictures or objects to support your words.
- [ ] Avoid arguing or correcting. Respond to the feeling behind the words.
- [ ] Speak to the person, not about them in front of them.

> **Practical tip:** If the person becomes upset, try to name the feeling gently ("You seem worried") and offer comfort, before trying to explain or fix the facts.

## Checklist 3: Create a calm, safe environment

A quiet setting reduces overload.

- [ ] Reduce background noise, such as a radio and television playing together.
- [ ] Keep rooms well lit, and avoid shadows and glare.
- [ ] Keep floors clear of loose items, rugs and trip hazards.
- [ ] Put familiar objects and photographs where the person can see them.
- [ ] Label or mark important places, such as the toilet, with clear signs or pictures.
- [ ] Keep medicines, sharp tools and cleaning products stored safely.
- [ ] Consider safety around doors and gates if the person may wander, and never leave them alone in an unsafe situation.

## Checklist 4: Protect dignity and independence

Dementia changes many things, but a person remains a person.

- [ ] Let the person do what they safely can, even if it is slower.
- [ ] Offer choices about clothes, food and activities.
- [ ] Help with personal care privately and respectfully.
- [ ] Keep involving them in family life and conversation.
- [ ] Draw on life history, such as work, songs, food and faith, to spark connection.

Some of these ideas overlap with keeping the mind active. See our article on [keeping older people mentally stimulated and socially connected](/blog/article-keeping-older-people-mentally-stimulated-and-connected).

## Checklist 5: Look after the caregiver

Caring for a person living with dementia is demanding, and caregivers need support too.

- [ ] Share tasks among family members and neighbours.
- [ ] Take regular breaks, even short ones.
- [ ] Talk with others who understand, such as a community or faith group.
- [ ] Ask a health professional or social worker about local support.
- [ ] Be kind to yourself. You will have hard days, and that is normal.

## When should you seek professional help?

Contact a doctor or nurse if the person shows sudden confusion, a rapid change in behaviour, new pain, a fall or refusal to eat or drink. Sudden change can be caused by an infection or other treatable problem. Always ask a professional before changing any medicine.
