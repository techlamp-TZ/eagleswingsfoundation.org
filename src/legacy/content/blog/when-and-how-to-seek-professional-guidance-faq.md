Caregivers should seek professional guidance when a change lasts, keeps returning, worries them, or affects daily life, and immediately in an emergency. You do not need to be certain that something is serious before you ask. This article answers common questions in plain language. It is general information and not medical advice.

## When should I seek help?

Think about seeking help if you notice any of these:

- A new change in behaviour, sleep, eating, mobility or mood that continues.
- A loss of skills the person used to have.
- Pain, discomfort or distress that you cannot explain or ease.
- Repeated falls or new safety worries.
- Your own exhaustion, low mood or worry. You count too.

Do not wait for a crisis. Early conversations are often easier and give more options. The World Health Organization notes that many people with disabilities face barriers to health services, so asking early can matter.

## What counts as an emergency?

Sudden, severe or frightening changes need urgent help. Go to the nearest health facility or contact emergency services. Do not wait for an appointment and do not rely on advice from an article. If you are unsure whether something is an emergency, treat it as one and ask for help.

## Who can I turn to?

Different professionals help with different needs.

| Professional | How they may help |
|---|---|
| Doctor or nurse | Examine the person, discuss health concerns, refer to specialists and manage medicine |
| Therapist | Guide exercises and skills that support movement, communication or daily activity |
| Teacher or school staff | Adjust learning and share what they see in the classroom |
| Social worker or counsellor | Advise on family support, wellbeing and practical help |
| Community or faith leader | Offer encouragement and connect families with others |

Each role is different. A teacher or a community leader cannot replace a medical assessment, but may help you take the next step.

## How do I prepare for a visit?

Preparation makes a short visit more useful.

1. **Write down your concerns.** List what you have noticed, when it started and what makes it better or worse.
2. **Bring your notes.** A simple daily record is very helpful. See [how to keep a simple daily observation log](/blog/article-keeping-a-simple-daily-observation-log).
3. **List medicines and supplements.** Bring the containers or a written list.
4. **Write your questions.** People often forget them in the room.
5. **Bring another person** if you can, for support and a second pair of ears.
6. **Prepare the person:** explain where you are going and why, in simple words.

> **Practical tip:** Ask, "Can you explain that in simpler words?" and "What should I watch for at home?" Professionals want you to understand.

## What should I ask during the visit?

- What do you think is happening, and what are the next steps?
- What can we do at home to help?
- What signs mean we should come back quickly?
- Is there anyone else who could help, such as a therapist or specialist?
- Can you write the plan down for me?

Ask about medicine only from the doctor or nurse who prescribed it. Never stop, start or change medicine without their advice.

## What if I do not understand or agree?

You may ask for clarification, or for a second opinion. Do so politely and openly. It is reasonable to want to understand the plan. If language, distance or cost is a barrier, say so, and ask what options exist. Do not assume prices or services, because these vary, and check locally.

## Should the person be part of the decision?

Yes. A person with a disability or an older person has a right to be heard about their own care. The UN [Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities.html), in force since 2008, is built on dignity, autonomy and participation. Speak to the person, not only about them. Explain what will happen and ask what worries them.

## What about my own wellbeing?

Caregiving can be tiring. If you feel overwhelmed, hopeless or unwell, tell a health worker or a trusted person. That is not a failure. Our perspective piece, [why caregivers need support too](/blog/article-why-caregivers-need-support-too), explores this in more depth.

Sharing tasks across relatives can also protect your health. See our [checklist for sharing care tasks across a family](/blog/article-sharing-care-tasks-across-a-family-checklist).
