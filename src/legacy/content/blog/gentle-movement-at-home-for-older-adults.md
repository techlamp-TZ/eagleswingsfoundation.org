Gentle movement at home can help an older person stay strong, steady and independent. Begin with a health check, choose safe and familiar movements, and build up slowly. This guide explains how, using activity advice from the World Health Organization (WHO).

> **Good to know:** This is general education, not medical advice. Please check with a doctor, nurse or physiotherapist before starting, especially if the person has a heart, lung, joint or balance problem, has recently been ill, or has had a fall.

## Why does movement matter in older age?

Regular movement supports muscles, bones, balance, sleep and mood. It also helps people keep doing daily tasks such as getting up from a chair, walking to the market or climbing steps. Being inactive for long periods makes these tasks harder over time.

The WHO says adults, including adults living with disability, should do at least [150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/physical-activity) per week. Older adults should also add balance and strength activities, about 3 days a week. Not everyone can reach these amounts, and the WHO is clear that some activity is better than none.

## What do you need before you begin?

You do not need equipment. Gather a few simple things:

- A sturdy chair without wheels, placed against a wall.
- A clear, dry, well-lit space with no loose mats or cables.
- Closed, flat, non-slip shoes, or bare feet on a non-slippery floor if a professional has advised it.
- A cup of water and a place to rest.
- Another person nearby, especially at the start.

## Step-by-step: a gentle routine

### Step 1: Talk with a health professional

Ask which movements are suitable and which to avoid. Mention any pain, dizziness, heart condition, joint replacement or medicine that may affect balance. Write down the advice.

### Step 2: Warm up while seated

Sit tall in the chair. Slowly roll the shoulders, turn the head gently from side to side, circle the wrists and ankles, and open and close the hands. Breathe normally. Warming up prepares the body and lets you notice how the person feels that day.

### Step 3: Walk or march

Walking is a natural way to build the aerobic activity the WHO recommends. Walk around the house, along a veranda or in a safe yard. If walking is hard, march gently on the spot while seated. Aim for a pace where the person can still talk.

### Step 4: Add strength

Strength work can use everyday actions:

1. Stand up from a chair and sit down slowly, using the arms if needed.
2. Lift a light, familiar object, such as a small water bottle, to shoulder height while seated.
3. Rise onto the toes while holding the back of the chair.
4. Press the hands gently against the wall, as if doing a standing push-up.

Do a few repeats and rest. Increase slowly over weeks, not days.

### Step 5: Practise balance

Always hold a stable support and have someone close by.

- Stand with feet together and hold on. Then let go with one finger only if it feels safe.
- Shift weight slowly from one foot to the other.
- Walk heel to toe along a wall, holding it lightly.

Balance work is most useful for lowering the risk of falls, which is why the WHO includes it for older adults.

### Step 6: Cool down and stretch gently

Finish with slow walking or seated breathing. Stretch the calves, arms and back gently, without bouncing or forcing. Offer water.

## When should you stop?

Stop and seek help if the person feels chest pain, sharp or new pain, dizziness, faintness, unusual breathlessness, or confusion. If it feels like an emergency, get medical help right away. Do not encourage anyone to push through discomfort.

## How can you make it a habit?

Attach movement to things that already happen. Stretch after morning tea. Walk after a meal. Put on music the person loves. Invite a grandchild or neighbour to join in, because company makes the routine more enjoyable and safer.

Celebrate effort rather than performance. A person who moves a little each day is doing something worthwhile. If interest fades, change the activity. Gardening, sweeping the yard, and light dancing all count as movement.
