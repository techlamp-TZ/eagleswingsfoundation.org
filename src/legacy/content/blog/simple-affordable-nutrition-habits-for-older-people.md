Good nutrition in older age does not need special or costly foods. It needs variety, regular meals, enough water and a pleasant place to eat. Below are answers to the questions families ask most often, based on World Health Organization (WHO) healthy diet guidance.

## What does the WHO say a healthy diet includes?

The WHO describes a healthy diet as one that protects against malnutrition in all its forms and against long-term conditions. In practice, that means eating a variety of foods:

- Vegetables and fruit, ideally fresh and in season.
- Legumes such as beans, lentils and peas.
- Nuts and whole grains, such as unrefined maize, millet or brown rice.
- Moderate amounts of fish, eggs, milk or meat.

It also means limiting salt, free sugars and unhealthy fats, and drinking safe water. This is general guidance for the whole population, and it suits most older people well.

## Are older people's food needs different?

The broad pattern is the same, but circumstances often change with age. Appetite may fall. Teeth or gums may hurt. Some medicines change taste or cause a dry mouth. Cooking or shopping may become tiring. Thirst may be less obvious.

These are reasons to adapt how food is prepared and served, not to give up on variety. If you notice a sudden change in eating, or weight loss the person did not intend, mention it to a doctor or nurse.

## How can we eat well without spending much?

Affordable eating is mostly about planning and simplicity.

### Cook with what is in season

Seasonal vegetables and fruit tend to be fresher and cheaper. Buying from a local market or growing a few plants, even in containers, adds variety.

### Lean on legumes and whole grains

Beans, lentils and grains are filling, flexible and good value. They combine well in stews and porridges.

### Waste less

Use leftovers in the next meal. Add vegetables to sauces and soups. Store food safely so it does not spoil.

### Limit costly extras

Sweet drinks and packaged snacks can be expensive and offer little nourishment. Keep them for occasional treats.

> **Practical tip:** Cook a larger pot of a nourishing dish, keep a portion safely for the next day, and serve it warm with a fresh vegetable or fruit on the side.

## What can help when appetite is low?

Try a gentle, patient approach.

1. Offer small meals and snacks several times a day rather than three big plates.
2. Serve favourite, familiar dishes, and make food look and smell inviting.
3. Add flavour with herbs, lemon or spices rather than extra salt.
4. Sit and eat together, since company often helps people eat more.
5. Keep a drink within reach and offer water regularly.

Never force-feed. If the person coughs or chokes when eating, or food seems to get stuck, see a health professional promptly, because swallowing difficulty needs proper assessment.

## How do teeth and gums affect eating?

Pain, loose teeth or poorly fitting dentures can make chewing hard and lead people to avoid nutritious foods. The WHO highlights oral health as part of overall health. Softer preparations, such as mashed beans, soft-cooked vegetables and soups, can help in the meantime. A dentist or oral health worker can treat the underlying problem.

## How much should a person drink?

There is no single right amount for everyone, so ask a health professional if the person has a heart, kidney or other condition. As a general habit, offer safe drinking water regularly through the day, and include soups and fruit that contain water. Watch for signs that someone is not drinking enough, such as unusual tiredness, confusion or very dark urine, and seek advice.

## How does mealtime affect mood?

Meals are social moments. Eating with family or neighbours gives structure to the day and a chance to talk. For older people who live alone, an invitation to share a meal can matter as much as the food itself.
