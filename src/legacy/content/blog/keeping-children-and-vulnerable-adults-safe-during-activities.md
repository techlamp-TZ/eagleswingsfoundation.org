To keep children and vulnerable adults safe during activities, plan ahead, keep supervision open and visible, have at least two responsible adults present, take extra care around water and outings, and record who attends. This guide walks through these steps. It is general good practice, not legal or medical advice, so seek qualified input for your own situation.

## Why do activities need a safety plan?

Activities are where people meet, learn and enjoy themselves. They are also where accidents and misunderstandings can happen, especially when groups include children, older people or people with disabilities who may need extra support.

The World Health Organization recommends regular physical activity for people of all ages and abilities, including an average of 60 minutes a day for children and adolescents. Being active is good for people. Safety planning simply makes sure that it happens in a way that protects them.

## Step 1: Know who is coming and what support they need

Before the day, ask families and carers what each person needs. Keep questions simple:

- Does the person need help with movement, communication or eating?
- Are there things that upset or frighten them?
- Are there health needs the team should know about?
- Who should we contact in an emergency?

Listen to the person as well as their family. Never make sharing this information a condition of taking part, and keep it private.

## Step 2: Set roles and ratios

Decide who leads, who watches, and who is the welfare contact. Make sure there are enough helpers for the size of the group and the level of need. If the activity is more demanding, or the group needs more support, add more helpers.

## Step 3: Have at least two adults present

A widely used good practice is that at least two responsible adults are present whenever children or people who need support are taking part. This helps in several ways:

1. It reduces the chance of any adult being alone and unobserved with a child.
2. It protects helpers from false or mistaken accusations.
3. One adult can help in an emergency while the other stays with the group.
4. It makes it easier to notice concerns.

If personal care such as toileting is needed, agree in advance how it will be handled respectfully, with the family's knowledge and in line with the organisation's rules.

> **Practical tip:** Choose spaces where activity can be seen, such as open rooms or areas with clear sight lines. Avoid closed rooms with nobody else nearby.

## Step 4: Take extra care with water

Water is one of the highest-risk settings, even in shallow areas. A person can be in difficulty quickly and quietly.

- Assign helpers whose only job is watching the water.
- Keep groups small and stay within arm's reach of those who need it.
- Follow the venue's rules and use trained lifeguards or instructors where they are available.
- Never leave anyone in or near water without an adult present.
- Ask families about swimming ability and comfort, and never pressure anyone to enter the water.
- Take advice from qualified professionals for any therapy in water.

## Step 5: Plan outings carefully

Outings add travel, crowds and unfamiliar places.

1. Visit or research the place first and check access, toilets and quiet areas.
2. Tell families the destination, timings and contact details in advance.
3. Agree a meeting point in case someone becomes separated.
4. Count the group at every stop, including when leaving and boarding transport.
5. Carry a charged phone, a list of contacts and a basic first-aid kit.

## Step 6: Record attendance

Write down who arrives and who leaves. Use a simple list, checked at the start, during any change of location, and at the end. Note who collected each child or person, so you know they left with someone they were expected to leave with.

Keep the list private, share it only with those who need it, and keep it secure. Our article on [protecting families' personal information online](/blog/article-protecting-families-personal-information-online) has more on handling records.

## Step 7: Know what to do if something goes wrong

- Make the person safe first, and call emergency services if needed.
- Tell the welfare contact and the person's family as soon as it is appropriate.
- Write down what happened, factually and privately.
- Follow the organisation's process for concerns, described in [how to raise a safeguarding concern safely](/blog/article-how-to-raise-a-safeguarding-concern-safely).

## A note on dignity during safety routines

Safety measures should never feel like punishment. Explain to participants, in words they can follow, why helpers stay close, why a list is taken and why everyone counts at each stop. Offer choices where you can, and speak to the person rather than about them. Some people, including autistic people and people living with dementia, may find changes of place or routine unsettling, so a calm, predictable approach helps. Ask families what reassures the person and use it.

## Step 8: Review afterwards

Spend a few minutes with the team after each activity. Ask what worked, what felt risky and what to change. Keep improving.
