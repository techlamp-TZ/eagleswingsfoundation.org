An accessible community event is one that people with different abilities can reach, understand and enjoy without having to ask for special favours. This checklist uses the ideas of universal design, promoted by the Centre for Excellence in Universal Design, to help you plan. It is general guidance, and it is not a substitute for advice from accessibility professionals or the people who will attend.

## What is universal design, and why use it for events?

Universal design means making places, information and services usable by as many people as possible from the start. It avoids designing for an imaginary "average" person.

The World Health Organization estimates that [about 1.3 billion people, around 16% of the world's population,](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/disability-and-health) experience significant disability. Many more people benefit from accessible design: an older person with tired legs, a parent with a pushchair, someone with a temporary injury. The UN [Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities.html) names accessibility, participation and inclusion as core principles, and events are a practical place to apply them.

Use the checklist below as a planning tool. Tick what you can do, and write down honestly what you cannot.

## Venue and getting there

Look at the whole journey, from the road to the seat.

- [ ] Is there a route without steps, or a clear plan for people who cannot use steps?
- [ ] Are paths wide, level and free from clutter, loose cables and wet patches?
- [ ] Is there somewhere to sit and rest, indoors or in shade?
- [ ] Are toilets reachable, with enough space for a helper or mobility aid?
- [ ] Is there shade, shelter and drinking water for hot or rainy weather?
- [ ] Have you described transport options and drop-off points in your invitation?

If the venue falls short, say so in advance. Accurate information lets people decide, and may help you find a better location next time.

## Information before the event

Many barriers appear before anyone arrives.

- [ ] Is the invitation written in plain, short sentences?
- [ ] Is key information offered in more than one format, such as printed, spoken and by phone?
- [ ] Have you said clearly who is welcome, including children, older people and carers?
- [ ] Do you give a simple running order, so people know what will happen?
- [ ] Is there a named contact person and phone number for questions?
- [ ] Have you invited people to share support needs, without making it compulsory?

## Communication on the day

- [ ] Are signs large, clear and placed at eye height?
- [ ] Do speakers use microphones if they are available, face the audience and speak at a steady pace?
- [ ] Are pictures or symbols used alongside words where they help?
- [ ] Do helpers introduce themselves and explain what they are doing?
- [ ] Is there time for questions and for people to answer at their own pace?

> **Good to know:** Speaking directly to a person with a disability, not to their companion, is a simple sign of respect. Offer help, but wait for a reply before assisting.

## Programme and activities

- [ ] Can activities be joined in more than one way, for example seated or standing?
- [ ] Are instructions demonstrated as well as explained?
- [ ] Are there breaks, and is the pace flexible?
- [ ] Is there a quiet corner for anyone who needs a rest from noise or crowds?
- [ ] Is nobody singled out, or made to perform in front of others, unless they want to?

For more on calming the environment, see [making community events sensory-friendly](/blog/article-making-community-events-sensory-friendly).

## Welcome, attitudes and helpers

Physical access is not enough if people feel unwelcome.

- [ ] Have helpers been briefed to be patient, respectful and ready to listen?
- [ ] Do they use person-respectful language, such as "person with a disability" or "autistic person"?
- [ ] Do they know where the quiet space, first aid contact and toilets are?
- [ ] Is there a simple way for people to give feedback or raise a concern?

## Comparing common barriers and simple fixes

| Barrier | Simple fix |
| --- | --- |
| Steps at the entrance | Use a level route or a step-free alternative and tell people in advance |
| Crowded seating | Leave clear spaces and aisles for mobility aids and helpers |
| Loud background noise | Reduce music, offer a quieter area and warn people about noisy moments |
| Long, complex instructions | Use short steps, demonstrate and repeat |
| Unclear signs | Use large text, symbols and helpers who can guide |

## After the event: check and learn

Ask attendees, carers and helpers what worked and what did not. Ask kindly and give people freedom to skip questions. Record two or three improvements for next time, and share the checklist with future organisers. Our guide on [gathering feedback without exploiting stories](/blog/article-collecting-event-feedback-without-exploiting-stories) explains how to do this with respect.
