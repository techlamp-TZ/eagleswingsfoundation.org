People who care for a child with a disability, or for an older person living with dementia, are doing skilled, tiring and often unseen work. Our view at Eagle's Wings is simple. Caregivers are not extras in the story of care. They are central to it, and they deserve support of their own.

## The work that is often unseen

Care does not end when the visitors go home. It is meals, washing, watching, listening, planning, calming and worrying, day after day. Much of it goes unnoticed because it happens inside the home and is done out of love.

The World Health Organization estimates that [1.3 billion people, about 16% of the world's population,](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/disability-and-health) experience significant disability, and that [more than 55 million people](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/dementia) live with dementia. Behind these numbers stand families, and in many families one or two people carry much of the daily load.

This is an opinion piece, and it draws on values, not on stories about specific people. We are not describing anyone's experience. We are describing why a fair approach to care includes the carer.

## Why is it hard to ask for help?

Many caregivers hesitate to ask. Some of the reasons are understandable:

- A feeling that no one else can do it properly.
- Worry about being seen as weak or ungrateful.
- Love and duty, which make rest feel like a betrayal.
- Not knowing what help exists.

In many communities, including in Tanzania, families and neighbours have long shared care. That tradition is a strength. But when families are busy, spread out or short of money, one person can end up alone. Then the tradition needs to be renewed, not assumed.

## Self-care without guilt

Self-care is a phrase that can sound like a luxury. It is not. It is maintenance, like feeding a fire so it keeps burning.

### What self-care can look like

Self-care does not need money or free days. It can be small:

- Eating regular meals and drinking enough water.
- Sleeping when the chance comes.
- Taking a short walk, if you are able.
- Talking with a friend or relative.
- Praying, singing or sitting quietly.
- Seeing a doctor or nurse about your own health.

The WHO recommends regular physical activity for adults, at least [150 to 300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/physical-activity) per week. Take that as a general target and speak to a health professional about what suits your own body.

### Letting go of guilt

Guilt often says: "If I rest, something will go wrong." A better thought is: "If I never rest, I may not be able to go on." Rest is part of the plan, not a break from it.

> **Good to know:** Feeling tired, sad or resentful at times is common among caregivers. It does not mean you are failing. If these feelings are heavy or last a long time, speak to a doctor, nurse or counsellor.

## Support is more than advice

Real support has several forms:

| Kind of support | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Practical | Someone takes over a meal, a school run or an evening |
| Emotional | Someone listens without judging |
| Informational | Someone explains the condition, the options and the next steps |
| Professional | A doctor, nurse or therapist gives guidance for the individual |
| Skills | Training that builds confidence in daily care |

No single person provides all five. That is why sharing matters. Our [checklist for sharing care tasks across a family](/blog/article-sharing-care-tasks-across-a-family-checklist) offers a starting point for that conversation.

## Why does this matter to the person being cared for?

A rested, supported caregiver can be patient, attentive and kind. An exhausted caregiver may struggle, through no fault of their own. Supporting the carer therefore protects the person receiving care too. It also supports dignity on both sides, because nobody should be pushed beyond their limits.

The UN [Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities.html), in force since 2008, is built on dignity, autonomy and inclusion. We believe that the same spirit applies to families. A person with a disability has a right to be valued, and their caregivers deserve to be treated with respect too.

## What can we each do?

- **Families:** Notice who is carrying the load. Offer a specific task, not just "let me know".
- **Neighbours and community:** Visit, listen and share a meal.
- **Caregivers:** Name one small thing that would help and ask for it.
- **Everyone:** Speak of caregiving as work worth recognising.

For practical routines that include rest, see [supporting families with consistent care routines](/blog/article-supporting-families-with-consistent-care-routines).
