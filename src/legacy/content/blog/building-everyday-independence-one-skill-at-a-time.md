Independence is not a single milestone. It is a collection of small skills, such as folding clothes, sweeping a floor or asking for help, that a person practises until they feel confident. When support is patient and respectful, each skill adds to a person's choice and dignity.

## What do we mean by life skills?

Life skills are the practical abilities that let a person take part in everyday life. They are often grouped into a few areas:

- **Daily living:** washing, dressing, preparing simple meals, doing laundry, cleaning and tidying.
- **Communication:** expressing needs, making choices, greeting people and asking for help.
- **Community participation:** using shared spaces, following a routine, moving safely, and joining group activities.
- **Self-care and wellbeing:** personal hygiene, rest, and noticing how the body and feelings are doing.

None of these areas is more important than the others. A young person may be confident with gardening and still need time with money or travel. That is normal for everyone.

## Why does independence matter?

Independence is closely linked to dignity. The UN [Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities.html), adopted in 2006 and in force since 2008, is built on principles such as autonomy, participation and inclusion. Learning life skills is one practical way to bring those principles into daily life.

The World Health Organization notes that [about 1 in 100 children worldwide](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/autism-spectrum-disorders) has autism, and that support needs vary greatly. Some autistic people live largely independently, while others need daily support. Both are valid. The aim is not to make anyone "less autistic". The aim is to widen a person's choices.

Note that both "autistic person" and "person with autism" are used by different people. It is polite to ask which a person prefers.

## How do skills actually grow?

Skills rarely appear from one explanation. They usually develop through steps that repeat.

### Start with what matters to the person

A skill is easier to learn when it connects to something the person cares about. Someone who loves plants may enjoy watering and weeding. Someone who likes a clean, orderly room may be motivated by mopping. Interests are a doorway to learning.

### Break tasks into small parts

Big tasks can feel overwhelming. Splitting a task into small, clear steps makes it easier to learn and to teach. Our article on [breaking a chore into small teachable steps](/blog/article-breaking-a-chore-into-small-teachable-steps) shows how.

### Practise in real settings

Practising laundry with real clothes, or watering with a real garden, teaches more than a demonstration. Real settings also help skills carry over into everyday life.

### Fade support slowly

Support may begin with hand-over-hand help, then move to a gesture, then a picture, then only a nearby presence. Each person moves at their own speed, and moving back a step is not failure.

## What does helpful support look like?

The table below compares two common approaches.

| Approach that builds independence | Approach that can limit it |
| --- | --- |
| Offers choices and waits for a response | Decides for the person |
| Teaches one step at a time | Demonstrates the whole task once |
| Celebrates effort and progress | Focuses only on mistakes |
| Reduces help gradually | Removes help suddenly or never |
| Respects privacy and preferences | Ignores how the person feels |

> **Good to know:** Doing a task for someone is sometimes faster, but it can quietly teach them that they are not expected to try. A little extra time now often creates more choice later.

## How can families begin at home?

Home is often the best classroom. A few gentle ideas:

1. Pick one small task that fits the household, such as carrying a bucket, wiping a table or feeding a pet.
2. Do the task at the same time each day, so it becomes a routine.
3. Use words, pictures or demonstration in the way that works best for the person.
4. Give the person a real role, so the task has meaning for the family.
5. Notice and name what went well.

Extended families and neighbours can help too. In many Tanzanian communities, children and young people already share household tasks. Making room for a young person with a disability to share in these tasks is a simple form of inclusion.

## What should we remember about safety and rights?

Skills should never be taught in ways that are unsafe or humiliating. Watch for tiredness and stress. Keep hot water, sharp tools and chemicals under adult supervision until the person and their support team agree it is safe. If you have questions about a particular young person's needs, speak with a doctor, therapist or other qualified professional.

Also remember that a person has the right to say no, to rest, and to change their mind. Independence includes choosing.
