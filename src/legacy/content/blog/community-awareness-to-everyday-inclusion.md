Awareness is knowing that disability exists and that people face barriers. Inclusion is what happens next: those barriers get smaller, and people take part in daily life with the same dignity as everyone else. This article is a viewpoint on how communities can make that move.

## Why is awareness not enough?

Awareness days, posters and talks have value. They start conversations and break silence. But a person with a disability does not live inside a campaign. They live in a street, a classroom, a congregation and a household.

If a child understands that a classmate has a disability but the classroom still has no room for that classmate to take part, nothing has changed for the classmate. Knowing is the first step. Changing habits and spaces is the second, and it is the harder one.

The World Health Organization estimates that [about 1.3 billion people, roughly 16% of the world's population,](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/disability-and-health) experience significant disability. That is a large share of every community. Including them is not a special project. It is part of building a community that works for everyone.

## What does everyday inclusion look like?

Inclusion is easiest to recognise in ordinary moments. It is less about big gestures and more about who is expected, who is asked and who is missed.

- A neighbour is greeted by name, not spoken about over their head.
- A meeting is explained in plain words, and questions are welcome.
- A child with a disability is invited to the game, the prayer group or the birthday, and helped to join in if they want.
- A family caring for someone at home is asked what would help, rather than told what they should do.
- A path, doorway or seat is planned with different bodies in mind from the start.

None of these needs a large budget. They need attention and the habit of asking, "Who might be left out here, and what would help?"

## Inclusion versus a seat in the room

There is a difference between being present and taking part. A person can sit in a room and still have no voice, no role and no one speaking to them. Real participation has three parts.

### Access

The person can physically and practically get there, and can understand what is happening. That may mean a ramp, a quieter space, clear language or someone who explains.

### Voice

The person is asked for their views and those views change decisions. Where a person cannot easily speak for themselves, the people who know them well are listened to, and the person's own signs and preferences are respected.

### Contribution

The person is seen as someone who gives as well as receives. Every person has strengths, interests and something to offer, whether it is a skill, a sense of humour, a task they enjoy or simply being a good friend.

## Who is missing from the conversation?

Too often, decisions about people with disabilities are made without them. Families and caregivers are sometimes left out as well, even though they carry much of the daily work.

One helpful test for any community plan is to ask who was in the room when it was made. If people with disabilities, older people and caregivers were not, the plan probably has gaps. The disability rights movement has long used the phrase "nothing about us without us", and it is a good rule for any group that wants to include others.

> **Good to know:** The UN [Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities.html), adopted in 2006 and in force since 2008, is built on dignity, participation and inclusion. It gives communities a shared language for why this matters. You can read our plain-language guide in [the CRPD explainer](/blog/article-un-convention-rights-persons-with-disabilities-explained).

## How do workplaces and livelihoods fit in?

Belonging is not only social. Having meaningful work, or a way to earn, is part of independence and respect. The International Labour Organization's material on disability and work is a useful starting point for employers and community groups thinking about inclusive workplaces. It treats decent work for people with disabilities as a matter of rights and of good practice for everyone.

For a small business or an informal trade, inclusion may look simple: a clear description of tasks, a willingness to adjust how something is done, and support that matches the person rather than assumptions about them. Employers and trainers should always seek advice from qualified professionals and from the person themselves about what support works.

## What can one community do this month?

Big change is built from small, repeated steps. A group does not need to wait for funding or a formal plan.

1. Choose one place where people gather, such as a school, a place of worship or a neighbourhood association.
2. Ask people with disabilities and their families what gets in the way, and listen without defending.
3. Pick one or two barriers that can be changed soon.
4. Change them, then ask again whether it helped.
5. Share what you learned so others can do the same.
