An older person's health is not only a matter of the body. A mind that is engaged and a life that includes other people are part of ageing well. Our view at Eagle's Wings is that older people should be treated as valued members of their community, with a continuing role, not as people to be managed.

## Why does the mind need exercise too?

We often ask an older relative about blood pressure, appetite and walking. We less often ask, "What are you interested in these days?" or "Who have you talked with this week?"

The World Health Organization (WHO) treats the mental health of older adults as an important part of healthy ageing. Life in later years can bring real changes: retirement, bereavement, reduced mobility, health problems and, sometimes, less contact with others. Meaningful activity and good relationships are among the things that help people stay well through those changes.

The United Nations and the WHO have named 2021–2030 the [Decade of Healthy Ageing](https://www.who.int/initiatives/decade-of-healthy-ageing). The message is that communities should help people stay able, active and included as they grow older.

## What is a perspective worth challenging?

A common assumption is that older people mostly need rest and quiet. Rest is important, but too much can leave a person feeling unneeded. A more respectful view is that older people continue to learn, teach, decide and belong.

In many Tanzanian families, elders hold knowledge of family history, farming, cooking, songs, proverbs and conflict resolution. Asking for that knowledge is a way of saying, "You still matter to us." This is a value, not a programme, and any family or neighbourhood can practise it.

## What does mental stimulation really mean?

It does not need to be a test or a puzzle. Mental stimulation is anything that engages attention, memory, imagination or conversation. Ideas include:

- Storytelling, where a grandparent shares memories and young people ask questions.
- Reading aloud, or listening to the radio and talking about it.
- Singing or listening to familiar songs.
- Games such as cards, board games or word games.
- Practical tasks, such as sorting vegetables, folding laundry or tending plants.
- Learning something new at any pace, such as a craft or a simple skill.

The best choice is the one that fits the person. A task that is too hard causes frustration, and one that is too easy can feel patronising. Adapt to the person's abilities and interests, and let them lead.

## Why does social connection matter?

People are social by nature. Regular conversation, shared meals and community gatherings support mood, give structure to the week and provide a chance to notice changes in health. Loneliness and isolation can weigh heavily on wellbeing, and they can affect people who live surrounded by family as well as those who live alone. What counts is the quality of contact, feeling heard and included.

> **Good to know:** A short, regular visit or call is often more helpful than an occasional long one. Predictable contact gives something to look forward to.

## How can families and communities help?

A few small actions make a difference:

1. **Ask before you assume.** Find out what the person enjoys and what they would like to keep doing.
2. **Include, do not just care for.** Invite older people to family decisions, celebrations and community events.
3. **Make it possible.** Think about transport, seating, lighting, hearing and toilets so that gatherings are accessible.
4. **Connect generations.** Encourage children and young people to spend time with elders and learn from them.
5. **Notice changes.** If an older person seems withdrawn, unusually forgetful or low in mood, encourage a check-up with a qualified health worker.

Some older people, including those living with dementia, need extra support to take part. Our checklist on [supporting a person living with dementia](/blog/article-supporting-a-person-living-with-dementia-routines-calm) covers routines and communication.

## What about mood and mental health?

Feeling sad at times is human. Persistent low mood, worry or withdrawal is different, and it deserves attention. It should not be dismissed as "just old age." A doctor or nurse can assess the person's mental and physical health and suggest support. If someone talks about harming themselves, seek urgent help from a health professional or emergency service.

Mind, body and mood are linked. A walk with a neighbour combines movement and company. A shared meal combines nutrition and conversation.
